The state case

State of Minnesota v. Chauvin, in Hennepin County, for murder.

  1. The jury decides · April 2021

    Three charges, one evening

    Did the State prove he committed a crime?

    Yes · guilty on all three, Apr 20, 2021

    This is the trial. The State charged him with three crimes for the same nine and a half minutes, and to convict on any of them the jury had to be sure of every piece beyond a reasonable doubt. Twelve people had to agree, which is what unanimous means, and they had to look at each charge separately.

    Two of the pieces are the same for all three charges, so they sit first inside this card. The third is what makes each charge different. All three are needed, so a no on any one of them for a charge means not guilty on that charge.

    Who must prove itThe State. He didn’t have to prove anything, and he didn’t testify.

    All three are needed

    Part one · The cause

    Did his actions cause George Floyd’s death?

    Yes · the jury found it

    This was the biggest fight in the trial. The jury was told his actions didn’t have to be the only cause, just a substantial causal factor. So heart disease or drugs playing a part wouldn’t clear him, unless one of them alone caused the death, which the law calls a superseding cause.

    The State’s doctors said Floyd died of a lack of oxygen from being held face down with a knee on his neck. The defense’s medical examiner said his heart gave out, with drugs and other things adding to it. The jury went with the State.

    and

    Part two · The force

    Was his force more than a reasonable officer would use?

    Yes · the jury found it

    Police are allowed to use force to arrest someone, so the jury was told no crime is committed if an officer uses reasonable force making a lawful arrest. That’s the authorized use of force, and the State had to prove his wasn’t.

    It’s judged the way a reasonable officer would see it at that moment, not with hindsight. The State said any force had to end once Floyd was handcuffed, face down and not resisting. The defense said you have to count the whole struggle before that and the angry crowd.

    and

    Part three · The charge

    Does what he did fit each of the three crimes?

    Yes · on all three

    Each charge needs something more, and none of them needs an intent to kill. Second-degree unintentional murder means he caused the death while committing a felony, here third-degree assault, which means meaning to use the force and the force causing serious harm. Third-degree murder means an act “eminently dangerous” to others with no regard for life. Second-degree manslaughter means consciously taking a big risk that an ordinary careful person would see.

    The jury said guilty on all three.

    If no

    Outcome

    Not guilty

    Then

    He walks out of court

    If the jury had a reasonable doubt about the cause or the force, that’s not guilty on every charge, and the State can’t appeal an acquittal. A doubt about one charge’s own piece would only clear him of that charge.

    That isn’t what happened. The jury agreed on all three by early afternoon on its second day.

    If yes

  2. The judge decides · June 2021

    Only one conviction, the most serious

    Should his sentence go past the guidelines?

    Yes · 270 months, Jun 25, 2021

    Guilty on three charges doesn’t mean three sentences. Minnesota only lets the court convict on the most serious one for the same act, so he was convicted of second-degree murder, and the other two verdicts were never entered. The state’s sentencing guidelines say that for him, with no record, the sentence should be 150 months, between 128 and 180.

    To go higher the judge needs an aggravating factor, something that makes this crime worse than the usual one. He gave up his right to have a jury decide those facts, which a ruling called Blakely gives him, so the judge did, and found four. He leaned on two, the particular cruelty and the abuse of his authority as an officer, and gave him 270 months, which is 22 and a half years. That’s an upward departure. Either way, the case would go on to an appeal.

    What each side askedThe State asked for 30 years. He asked for probation.

    Either way

  3. His appeal · 2022 to 2023

    Twelve claims, decided together

    Was his trial unfair?

    No · affirmed Apr 17, 2023

    After a conviction comes the direct appeal, where a higher court looks for mistakes in the trial. It doesn’t hear new evidence or decide again whether he did it. His lawyer gave the Court of Appeals twelve reasons, and these are the three that mattered most.

    Any one of them could have gotten him a new trial, so a yes on any one was enough. The court said no to all twelve, the sentence included. The Minnesota Supreme Court and the U.S. Supreme Court both refused to take the case after that, which ended it.

    A yes on any one is enough

    Claim one · The publicity

    Should the trial have been moved, delayed or the jury locked away?

    No · the jury was shown to be fair

    He said nobody in Minneapolis could judge him fairly, after the riots, the city’s $27 million settlement in the middle of jury selection, the courthouse wrapped in razor wire and soldiers, and a congresswoman telling protesters to get more confrontational.

    The court said publicity this big was going to follow the case anywhere, and what counts is whether the judge did enough to get a fair jury anyway. It said he did, with questionnaires, jurors questioned one at a time, extra strikes for the defense and jurors removed for cause.

    or

    Claim two · A juror

    Did a juror hide views that should have kept him off?

    No · his answers were true

    After the verdict, Juror 52 told a radio interviewer he’d been pulled over by Minneapolis police “probably about 50 times,” and it came out that he’d gone to a march in Washington in August 2020 where Floyd’s family spoke. Chauvin said he hid that in jury selection and asked for a hearing to question him.

    The court said his answers on the questionnaire were true as written, and Chauvin had three strikes left he could have used.

    or

    Claim three · The law

    Is it wrong to convict an officer of felony murder for force in an arrest?

    No · he can be convicted

    This one was about the law itself. Felony murder here rests on assault, and he said officers have to use force to arrest people, so the charge basically turns every officer into a criminal whenever someone dies.

    The court said no. Officers are only allowed reasonable force, so force that’s unreasonable can be an assault like anyone else’s. That ruling is now the law in Minnesota.

    If yes

    Outcome

    A new trial

    Then

    Maybe somewhere else

    The conviction would be thrown out and the State could try him again, maybe in another county. Not every mistake gets that. A court has to think it could have changed the result.

    None of the claims got that far.

    If no

  4. His petitions · 2024 to now

    The case is here

    After the appeal is over

    Is there a reason to reopen the case now?

    Undecided

    Once the appeals are done, a post-conviction petition is the last way back into a state court. It usually needs something the trial record didn’t have. And under the Knaffla rule, it can’t just argue again what was argued on appeal, or could have been.

    He’s filed three since 2024 with his new lawyer, Gregory Joseph. The first was dismissed in 2025. The other two are open, and they argue different things, so they’re the two cards inside this one. A yes on either would reopen the case.

    A yes on either one is enough

    Petition two · On appeal now

    Did the trial rest on wrong testimony or wrong instructions?

    No · denied May 1, 2026 · appealed

    This one says the State’s doctors drew their conclusions from video instead of medical findings, that the police chief and two trainers misdescribed what the department taught, backed by statements from 57 officers, and that the jury instructions were flawed.

    Judge Paul Scoggin said none of it warranted relief, and that most of it was or could have been argued on appeal. Chauvin appealed in June 2026, and the Court of Appeals hasn’t ruled.

    or

    Petition three · Filed August 2026

    Was he charged the wrong way from the start?

    Undecided

    This one isn’t about the evidence. It says the governor had no right to hand the case to the Attorney General, and that charging him without a grand jury let prosecutors decide on their own what a grand jury should have. He calls that a structural error, so basic that the whole case would have to go.

    He’s asked the Court of Appeals to pause his other appeal so the trial court can hear this one first. No ruling had been reported by October 22, 2026.

    If no

    Outcome

    The conviction stands

    Then

    He keeps serving his sentence

    If every petition fails, the conviction and the 270 months stay as they are, and the State never has to try him again.

    He’s serving the state sentence at the same time as the federal one, in federal prison.

    If yes

    Outcome

    Reopened

    Then

    A new trial, or the charges thrown out

    The second petition asks for a new trial or at least a hearing. The third asks for much more, that the conviction be vacated and the charges dismissed.

    Even then his federal conviction, which he pleaded guilty to, wouldn’t be touched by anything a state court does.

The federal case

United States v. Chauvin, in federal court, for violating Floyd’s civil rights. A separate prosecution with its own ending.

  1. United States v. Chauvin · St. Paul

    Decided by his own plea

    Did he violate George Floyd’s constitutional rights?

    Yes · his plea, Dec 15, 2021

    This is a different crime in a different court. The federal law makes it a crime for someone using the power of a government job, under color of law, to take away a person’s rights, here the right to be free from unreasonable force by police. It doesn’t matter that the state already convicted him, because the state and the federal government are separate governments with their own laws.

    There was no jury question here, because he answered it himself. He pleaded guilty, and admitted his force “became objectively unreasonable and excessive” once the officers decided not to tie Floyd’s legs. He also pleaded guilty over the 2017 arrest of a 14-year-old. The judge gave him 21 years, running at the same time as the state sentence.

    His plea dealGave up most appeals and most later challenges, except a claim that his lawyer failed him.

    Yes, by his plea

  2. His motion · Since November 2023

    The case is here

    The one challenge his deal allowed

    Should his guilty plea be undone?

    Undecided

    He says his lawyer, Eric Nelson, never told him that a pathologist had written to Nelson and the judge during the state trial with a theory that a rare tumor found at the autopsy, a paraganglioma, caused Floyd’s death. If he’d known, he says, he wouldn’t have pleaded guilty.

    That’s a claim of ineffective assistance, and to win it he needs both cards inside this one. The judge let his experts examine Floyd’s heart tissue in December 2024 and put the hearing on hold for the testing in January 2025. Nothing public has happened since.

    Both are needed

    Part one · His lawyer

    Did his lawyer fall short by not telling him?

    Undecided

    The question isn’t whether the tumor theory is right. It’s whether a reasonable lawyer would have told his client about it before he pleaded.

    The government says it wouldn’t have mattered anyway, because the autopsy findings came out at the state trial and the jury wasn’t persuaded by the same kind of argument.

    and

    Part two · The plea

    Would he have gone to trial if he’d known?

    Undecided

    He also has to show a real chance he’d have turned the deal down and gone to trial. He says he would have.

    At his plea hearing he told the judge he understood what he was giving up, and in his plea agreement he admitted that his actions kept Floyd from getting enough oxygen to live.

    If no

    Outcome

    The plea stands

    Then

    21 years, at the same time as the state sentence

    The federal conviction and sentence stay as they are, and his deal leaves him almost nothing else to bring.

    His state conviction is a separate question, for the state courts.

    If yes

    Outcome

    The plea is undone

    Then

    Back to the start of the federal case

    Undoing the plea wouldn’t clear him. It would put the federal charges back where they were before he pleaded, with a trial or a new deal ahead.

    It wouldn’t touch the state murder conviction either.

? Terms

Plain English

Words these pages use because the courts use them. Two courts convicted him, a state court and a federal one, and a few words belong to only one of them.

The courts

Hennepin County District Court
The state trial court for Minneapolis and the county around it. Judge Peter Cahill tried the case there. A different judge, Paul Scoggin, has heard his petitions since.
Minnesota Court of Appeals
The state court that hears appeals from trial courts. Three of its judges decide each case.
Minnesota Supreme Court
The state’s highest court. It chooses which appeals to hear, and it turned down his.
Federal court
The United States’ own courts, separate from the state’s. The federal case was in the U.S. District Court in St. Paul, before Judge Paul Magnuson.
Attorney General
Minnesota’s chief lawyer, elected statewide. Keith Ellison’s office took over the prosecution from the county prosecutor in May 2020.
Two prosecutions
A state and the federal government count as separate governments, so each can prosecute the same act under its own laws. That isn’t double jeopardy.

The paperwork

Complaint
The document that starts a criminal case in Minnesota. It lists the charges and the facts the prosecutor says support them. An amended complaint replaces it.
Grand jury
A group of citizens who hear the prosecutor’s evidence in private and decide whether to charge someone. Minnesota only requires one for crimes that carry life in prison, like first-degree murder, so this case started with a complaint instead.
Indictment
A charge brought by a grand jury. The federal case started with one.
Information
A charge a prosecutor files without a grand jury. In federal court that takes the defendant’s consent, which he gave for the 2017 count.
Docket
The court’s numbered list of everything filed in a case. Entry 42 is the forty-second thing filed.
Plea agreement
A written deal between a defendant and the prosecutors. He pleads guilty to some charges, and they agree on others to drop and on a range of sentence.
Broadcast transcript
A word-for-word record. The court reporter’s transcript of this trial was never posted, so these pages quote transcripts typed from the court’s own video feed and cite them by its clock.

The charges

Second-degree unintentional murder
Causing a death, without meaning to, while committing a felony. Here the felony was third-degree assault. This is the one count he was convicted and sentenced on.
Third-degree assault
Intentionally using unlawful force on someone, without their consent, that causes substantial bodily harm. He only had to mean to use the force, not to cause that much harm.
Third-degree murder
Causing a death by an act “eminently dangerous” to others, done with “a depraved mind,” without meaning to kill. Also called depraved-mind murder.
Second-degree manslaughter
Causing a death by culpable negligence, which means consciously taking a chance of causing death or great harm that an ordinary careful person would see.
Lesser offense
A crime contained in a more serious one. Minnesota law allows only one conviction for the same act, so the other two guilty verdicts were never entered as convictions.
Under color of law
Acting with the power of a government job, like a police officer on duty. The federal civil rights law makes it a crime to use that power to take away someone’s constitutional rights.
Aiding and abetting
Intentionally helping someone else commit a crime. The other three officers were charged that way.

How a case moves

Probable cause
Enough evidence to make it reasonable to believe a person committed the crime. It’s the bar a charge has to clear to go to trial, and it’s a lot lower than proof.
Venue
Where a trial is held. A defendant can ask to move it if publicity would make a fair jury impossible where the crime happened.
Continuance
Putting a trial or a hearing off to a later date.
Severance
Splitting one trial of several defendants into separate trials.
Sequester
Keeping jurors away from news and the public, sometimes in a hotel, so nothing outside the courtroom reaches them.
Jury selection
Questioning possible jurors one by one to find out if they can be fair. Also called voir dire.
Peremptory strike
Removing a possible juror without giving a reason. Each side gets a set number. Here the judge raised his to 18 and the State’s to 10.
Excused for cause
Removing a possible juror for a specific reason they can’t be fair, like having already made up their mind. There’s no limit on these.
Alternate juror
A juror who hears the whole trial but only deliberates if one of the twelve has to leave.
Anonymous jury
Jurors known in court only by number, their names kept from the public until the judge releases them.
Earlier incident
Evidence of something that happened before the case, which a jury may only use for a narrow purpose the judge sets. Minnesota calls it Spreigl evidence.
The Fifth
The right not to be a witness against yourself. A defendant who uses it doesn’t testify, and the jury is told it can’t hold that against him.
Rebuttal
Evidence or argument that answers the other side’s. The State goes last because it has to prove the case.
Beyond a reasonable doubt
The level of proof needed to convict. It means proof that leaves the jury firmly convinced, not proof beyond every possible doubt.
Unanimous
All twelve jurors agreeing. A verdict in a criminal case has to be.
Polling the jury
Asking each juror out loud, one at a time, if the verdict read is theirs.

What the jury had to decide

Substantial causal factor
The test for causing a death. His acts didn’t have to be the only cause, only a substantial one. Other causes don’t excuse him unless one of them alone caused the death.
Superseding cause
Something that comes afterward and breaks the chain, so that it alone causes the death. It’s the one way another cause can clear a defendant.
Authorized use of force
The defense the jury was told about. No crime is committed if a police officer uses reasonable force to make a lawful arrest, and the State had to prove his force wasn’t that.
Reasonable officer
How force is judged. The question is what a reasonable officer in the same situation would have done at the moment the force was used, not what the officer meant or what hindsight shows.
Prone restraint
Holding a person face down. Police training warns it can make it hard to breathe, and says to move the person onto their side once they’re under control.
Recovery position
Rolling a person onto their side so they can breathe more easily. The Minneapolis police’s own training said to do it.
Hobble
A strap that ties a person’s legs together. The officers talked about using one and didn’t.
Maximal restraint technique
The Minneapolis police method of strapping a struggling person’s legs and moving them off their stomach. The defense said what the officers did began as one. The State said the knee was no part of it.
Asphyxia
Death from not getting enough oxygen. The State’s doctors said the restraint caused it.
Paraganglioma
A rare tumor that can release adrenaline. One was found at the autopsy and called incidental, meaning the medical examiner didn’t think it played a part.

The sentence

Sentencing guidelines
Minnesota’s chart of how long a sentence should be for each crime, set by a state commission. A judge has to stay inside its range unless there are substantial and compelling reasons not to.
Presumptive sentence
What the guidelines call for. For this crime and no past record it’s 150 months, inside a range of 128 to 180.
Aggravating factor
A fact that makes a crime worse than the usual one of its kind, like particular cruelty. It has to be proved before a judge can go past the guidelines.
Blakely
A 2004 U.S. Supreme Court ruling that a jury has to find the facts behind a longer sentence, unless the defendant gives that up. He gave it up, so the judge decided them.
Upward departure
A sentence longer than the guidelines’ range. His was 270 months against a top of 180.
Concurrent
Served at the same time. His state and federal sentences run together, not one after the other.
Supervised release
A period after a federal prison term when a person lives under conditions the court sets, checked by a probation officer.

After the verdict

Motion for a new trial
Asking the trial judge to throw out the verdict and start over because something went wrong. It comes before any appeal.
Schwartz hearing
A hearing in Minnesota where a judge questions jurors about possible misconduct after a trial. He asked for one and was refused.
Direct appeal
The first appeal after a conviction, which looks only at what happened at trial. His was decided in 2023.
Petition for review
Asking the Minnesota Supreme Court to hear a case after the Court of Appeals has ruled. It turns most of them down.
Certiorari
Asking the U.S. Supreme Court to hear a case. It takes very few, and turning one down decides nothing about the case itself.
Post-conviction petition
A way to challenge a state conviction after the appeal is over, usually with something that wasn’t in the trial record. It goes back to the trial court first.
Knaffla rule
Minnesota’s rule that a petition after the appeal can’t raise what was raised, or could have been raised, on appeal, with narrow exceptions.
Structural error
A mistake in how a case was run that’s so basic a court doesn’t ask whether it changed the result. His lawyer says the way he was charged is one.
Motion to vacate
The federal version of a post-conviction petition, under section 2255 of the federal code. His asks to undo his federal guilty plea.
Ineffective assistance
A lawyer’s work falling so far short that it denied the client a fair process. It’s the one kind of claim his plea deal let him bring later.
Stay
Pausing a case, or a ruling, until something else is decided.

The stamp on a ruling

Granted
The court said yes to the request on this card, whichever side made it.
Granted in part
The court gave some of what was asked for on this card and said no to the rest.
Denied
The court said no to the request on this card.
Reinstated
A charge that had been dismissed was put back, so the jury could decide it.
Guilty
The jury’s verdict, or his own plea in court. It becomes a conviction once the court enters judgment on it.
Affirmed
The court above agreed with the court below and left the conviction and the sentence as they were.
Dismissed
Thrown out without a ruling on whether the claims in it were right.
