←  State v. Chauvin

Hennepin Cty. Dist. Ct., Minn. · No. 27‑CR‑20‑12646  ·  D. Minn. · No. 0:21‑cr‑00108

State v. Chauvin

TLDR View Chronological View Courtroom View Side by Side View

Chronological View

Prosecution · state or federal Court Defense Neither side

  1. May 25, 2020

    The evening at 38th Street and Chicago Avenue, as the courts later described it.

  2. Prosecution The side bringing the charges

    State of Minnesota

    Through the state Attorney General

    The case is brought in the name of the State of Minnesota. The Hennepin County Attorney filed the first charges on May 29, 2020, and two days later Gov. Tim Walz put the state’s Attorney General, Keith Ellison, in charge of the prosecution.

    Four of its lawyers sat in court at a time: two assistant attorneys general, Matthew Frank and Erin Eldridge, and two outside lawyers who worked for free, Jerry Blackwell and Steve Schleicher. Ellison came for the sentencing. On appeal the State was represented by Neal Katyal. In the federal case the prosecution was the United States.

    Complaint, May 29, 2020 · WCCO, May 31, 2020 · AP, Apr 4, 2021 · Sentencing order p. 1 · Ct. App. opinion p. 1

    Defendant The person charged

    Derek Chauvin

    Minneapolis police officer at the time

    Born in 1976, he had been a Minneapolis police officer for more than 18 years by May 2020. He also trained new officers on the street, and was training one the day of the 2017 arrest he later admitted using excessive force in.

    Eric Nelson defended him at trial and in the federal case, paid through a statewide police association’s legal defense fund. William Mohrman took his appeal. Since 2024 a federal public defender has handled his motion to undo his plea, and Gregory Joseph his petitions in state court. He’s in federal prison in Texas, serving both sentences at once.

    Amended complaint p. 1 · Indictment p. 1 · Plea agreement p. 7 · AP, Apr 4, 2021 · Ct. App. opinion p. 1 · ECF 555 · Star Tribune, Dec 4, 2025 · MPR News, Aug 18, 2026

    May 25, 2020

    A call about a fake $20 bill

    Just after 8 p.m. a clerk at Cup Foods calls 911 about a customer who paid with a counterfeit bill. Officers Thomas Lane and J. Alexander Kueng find George Floyd in a car across the street.

    Lane drew his gun at the driver’s window and yelled at Floyd to put his hands up. Floyd, crying, told him several times, “Please don’t shoot me.” The officers handcuffed him and walked him to their squad car, where he resisted getting in and said he was claustrophobic.

    A struggle followed at the car, and two more officers, Derek Chauvin and Tou Thao, arrived to help. The caller had told 911 that Floyd seemed to be under the influence of something, and two passengers had been in the car with him.

    Ct. App. opinion, Apr 17, 2023 pp. 3–4 · Order of Oct 21, 2020 pp. 16–20

  3. May 25, 2020

    Face down on the street

    At 8:19 p.m. the officers put Floyd on the pavement, handcuffed and face down. Chauvin presses his left knee into his neck and keeps it there until the ambulance crew is ready to move him.

    Floyd said again and again that he couldn’t breathe. About four minutes and 45 seconds in he went silent, and 53 seconds after that he stopped responding. An off-duty firefighter offered to help and was turned away. At 8:25 Kueng said he couldn’t find a pulse. Chauvin answered “huh” and kept his knee where it was.

    The appeals court put the whole restraint at about nine minutes and 29 seconds. A crowd on the sidewalk shouted at the officers to check his pulse and get off him, and a 17-year-old, Darnella Frazier, filmed it on her phone. Her video was seen around the world.

    Ct. App. opinion pp. 4–5 · Order of Oct 21, 2020 pp. 22–29 · Star Tribune, day-by-day recap

  4. May 25, 2020

    Pronounced dead at the hospital

    At 9:25 p.m. at Hennepin County Medical Center. The county’s medical examiner later rules the death a homicide.

    Dr. Andrew Baker gave the cause as “cardiopulmonary arrest complicating law enforcement subdual, restraint, and neck compression.” He listed heart disease, fentanyl and recent methamphetamine use as other significant conditions. His office noted that “homicide” on a death record is a medical finding, not a legal one about blame or intent.

    Those other conditions are where the defense would build its case about how Floyd died.

    Order of Oct 21, 2020 p. 29 · Autopsy report pp. 12–24

  5. Charged

    Four days later, the first charges. Three days after that, a bigger one.

  6. May 29, 2020

    Charged with murder and manslaughter

    The Hennepin County Attorney charges Chauvin with third-degree murder and second-degree manslaughter.

    The complaint quoted the medical examiner’s first findings, that the autopsy showed “no physical findings that support a diagnosis of traumatic asphyxia or strangulation,” and that the restraint, heart disease and “any potential intoxicants” likely combined. The defense would quote that line for months.

    No grand jury was used. In Minnesota a complaint is how most crimes are charged. Chauvin’s third petition, filed in 2026, says that was a mistake in a case like his.

    Complaint, May 29, 2020 p. 3

  7. May 31, 2020

    The Attorney General takes over

    Gov. Tim Walz puts the state’s Attorney General, Keith Ellison, in charge of the prosecution.

    Until then it had been the case of the Hennepin County Attorney, Mike Freeman. Floyd’s family had asked for Ellison to take it over.

    This is the step Chauvin’s third petition, filed in August 2026, calls illegitimate.

    WCCO, May 31, 2020 · MPR News, Aug 18, 2026

  8. Jun 3, 2020

    Second-degree murder added

    An amended complaint adds the charge he’d be convicted of. The other three officers are charged too, with aiding and abetting.

    That made three counts. The new one, second-degree unintentional murder, means causing a death without meaning to while committing a felony, and the felony here was third-degree assault. The other two were third-degree murder and second-degree manslaughter.

    The complaint said his knee was on Floyd’s neck for “8 minutes and 46 seconds in total.” That number changed once the videos were studied. At trial the State’s figure was nine minutes and 29 seconds.

    Amended complaint pp. 1–2, 4

  9. The year before trial

    A dropped charge, a televised trial ordered, and a fight over whether to bring the charge back.

  10. Aug 28, 2020

    He asks the court to dismiss the charges

    His lawyer, Eric Nelson, says there isn’t probable cause for any of them, and asks to move the trial out of Minneapolis.

    The defense memo said Floyd “most likely died from an opioid overdose,” and quoted notes of the medical examiner telling prosecutors an overdose label would have been “acceptable” if Floyd had been found dead at home with no other apparent cause.

    Nelson also asked the court to take the Hennepin County Attorney’s office off the case over its meeting with the medical examiner. The same day, the State gave notice that it would ask for a sentence longer than the guidelines allow.

    Defense memorandum, Aug 28, 2020 p. 22 · State’s notice, Aug 28, 2020

  11. Oct 7, 2020

    Out on a $1 million bond

    He leaves prison to wait for trial at home. He’d been held since the day he was charged.

    He would stay free until the verdicts were read, when the judge sent him back into custody on the spot.

    Notice of release, Oct 7, 2020 · Federal judgment p. 2

  12. Oct 21, 2020Granted in part

    Third-degree murder thrown out

    Judge Peter Cahill dismisses the third-degree murder count for lack of probable cause. Second-degree murder and manslaughter stay.

    Minnesota’s depraved-mind murder law covers acts “eminently dangerous to others,” and the judge read that as excluding acts aimed only at the person who died. Nothing showed Chauvin’s acts endangered anyone but Floyd, he found.

    His 107-page order sets out the evening minute by minute from the body cameras, and many of the times on these pages come from it. It was written at an early stage, where a court takes the facts in the light most favorable to the prosecution.

    Order of Oct 21, 2020 p. 2, 54–60

  13. Nov 4, 2020

    Cameras in the courtroom

    The judge lets the trial be televised and orders an anonymous jury. For now, all four officers will be tried together, in Minneapolis.

    The State didn’t consent to cameras. All four defendants asked for them. The rules the country watched under came from this order: up to three fixed cameras, no juror ever on screen, and no witness under 18 shown without written consent.

    The judge kept the trial in Hennepin County, finding that “no corner of the [s]tate of Minnesota has been shielded from pretrial publicity,” and said he’d look at it again if things changed.

    Camera order pp. 2–4 · Venue order, Nov 4, 2020 · Juror order, Nov 4, 2020 · Joinder order, Nov 4, 2020 · Ct. App. opinion p. 6

  14. Jan 11, 2021

    His trial is split from the other three

    Not even the building’s largest courtroom, C-1856, can hold four defendants and their lawyers six feet apart, the judge finds. He severs Chauvin’s case, and Chauvin will be tried alone, starting March 8.

    The judge turned down the State’s request to put everything off to June because of the pandemic. The other three officers’ trial was moved to August 2021.

    Order of Jan 11, 2021 pp. 3–4

  15. Feb 11, 2021Denied

    The judge won’t bring back third-degree murder

    Ten days earlier the appeals court upheld a depraved-mind murder conviction in another Minneapolis police case. The State asks to restore the count. The judge says that decision isn’t final yet.

    The other case was Mohamed Noor’s, an officer who shot a woman who had called 911. On February 1, 2021, the Court of Appeals held that third-degree murder can rest on an act aimed at one person. The State appealed the judge’s refusal to follow it.

    Order of Feb 11, 2021

  16. Mar 5, 2021Granted

    The appeals court says to follow Noor

    Its published decisions bind trial courts from the day they’re filed, it rules, and it sends the question back.

    Chauvin asked the Minnesota Supreme Court to step in, and on March 10 it declined.

    Ct. App. opinion, Mar 5, 2021 · Minn. S. Ct. order, Mar 10, 2021

  17. The trial

    Jury selection, then three weeks of evidence in a courtroom rebuilt for distancing, on television from start to finish.

  18. Mar 9, 2021

    Jury selection begins

    One possible juror at a time, questioned by the judge and then by each side. Each had filled out a long questionnaire months before.

    For jury selection the judge gave the defense 18 peremptory strikes and the State 10, more than the usual five and three. The questionnaire asked what each person had seen of the video and the protests, about Black Lives Matter and Blue Lives Matter, and about police reform.

    Jurors were known only by number. No camera ever showed one.

    Ct. App. opinion pp. 7, 17 · Trial management order

  19. Mar 11, 2021Reinstated

    Third-degree murder is back

    With jury selection already under way, the judge restores the count. The jury will decide three charges.

    Later in 2021 the Minnesota Supreme Court reversed Noor’s conviction and held that depraved-mind murder can’t rest on conduct aimed at one person. By then Chauvin had been convicted only of second-degree murder, so his third-degree verdict was never entered, and the appeals court declined to rule on it.

    Order of Mar 13, 2021 p. 2 · Ct. App. opinion p. 24

  20. Mar 12, 2021

    The city settles for $27 million

    Minneapolis settles the Floyd family’s wrongful-death lawsuit, in the middle of jury selection.

    Three days later Chauvin asked for a change of venue, a continuance or a jury locked away from the news. The judge called back the seven jurors already seated and questioned them about the settlement, and excused two for cause. The rest of his requests were turned down.

    Ct. App. opinion pp. 8–9

  21. Mar 23, 2021

    Fifteen jurors seated

    Twelve to decide the case, two alternates, and a fifteenth to be let go if everyone else stayed.

    The defense finished with three of its strikes unused. The fifteenth juror was released before opening statements, and the two alternates were told who they were only after closing arguments.

    NPR, Apr 20, 2021 · Ct. App. opinion p. 17

  22. Mar 24, 2021Granted in part

    A 2019 arrest, partly allowed

    The defense wants the jury to see a May 2019 arrest of Floyd, an earlier incident. The judge lets in a narrow part of it.

    In both arrests, the judge wrote, Floyd put drugs in his mouth as police approached, and after being handcuffed he “complains of problems breathing and calls out for his mother.” None of the four officers had been there in 2019.

    The jury could weigh it only on what drugs may have done to Floyd’s body. It was “not to be used as evidence of the character of George Floyd,” the judge told them.

    Order of Mar 24, 2021 p. 3 · Jury instructions p. 11

  23. Mar 29, 2021

    “The most important numbers you will hear in this trial are 9:29”

    Jerry Blackwell opens for the State. Chauvin “betrayed this badge,” he says, when he used excessive force on a man who was handcuffed and defenseless.

    Blackwell told the jury it would hear Floyd say “I can’t breathe” 27 times in the first four minutes and 45 seconds, and see him go still.

    The State’s first witnesses were the 911 dispatcher who watched on a city camera and called a sergeant, a store clerk who filmed, and Donald Williams, a mixed martial arts fighter who called the hold a “blood choke.”

    Tr. Opening, 02:00 · 04:15 · Star Tribune, day-by-day recap

  24. Mar 29, 2021

    “The evidence is far greater than nine minutes and 29 seconds”

    Eric Nelson opens for Chauvin: look at the whole evening, the struggle at the car, the drugs and Floyd’s heart.

    Chauvin “did exactly what he had been trained to do over the course of his 19 year career,” Nelson said. Three officers couldn’t get Floyd into the squad car, and a growing, angry crowd was seen as a threat.

    On the cause of death, he said the evidence would show a heart rhythm problem brought on by heart disease, with fentanyl and methamphetamine, and none of the “telltale signs of asphyxiation.”

    Tr. Defense opening, 01:55 · 17:23 · 20:39

  25. Mar 30 – 31, 2021

    The people on the sidewalk

    Darnella Frazier, who filmed the video, testifies, along with three other young witnesses, the off-duty firefighter and the store clerk who took the $20 bill.

    Frazier was 17 that night. “It’s been nights I’ve stayed up apologizing to George Floyd for not doing more,” she told the jury. A nine-year-old who was with her testified too, and Nelson didn’t question her.

    Genevieve Hansen, the firefighter, said, “There is a man being killed, and I would have been able to provide medical attention.” Nelson’s questions to the bystanders were about the crowd getting louder and angrier.

    Star Tribune, day-by-day recap

  26. Apr 5, 2021

    The police chief: “that should have stopped”

    Chief Medaria Arradondo tells the jury that once Floyd stopped resisting, and certainly once he was in distress, the force should have ended.

    That day and the next, the department’s own trainers said a knee on the neck of a handcuffed man isn’t what they teach, and that officers are trained to roll a person into the recovery position. Shown a photo of the hold, the former head of training, Katie Blackwell, said, “I don’t know what kind of improvised position this is.”

    Chauvin’s 2025 petition says those officers misdescribed the training. A judge turned that claim down in May 2026.

    Tr. Arradondo · Tr. Mercil · Star Tribune, day-by-day recap

  27. Apr 8 – 9, 2021

    The State’s doctors: he died from a lack of oxygen

    A lung specialist, an emergency physician and a retired medical examiner say the restraint, not drugs or heart disease, caused his death. A toxicologist says the drugs in his blood don’t look like an overdose.

    Dr. Martin Tobin, a pulmonologist, walked the jury through the video and said Floyd “died from a low level of oxygen.” Dr. Bill Smock said, “That is not a fentanyl overdose. That is someone begging to breathe.”

    Dr. Andrew Baker, who did the autopsy, stood by his finding of homicide and said the restraint and the neck compression were “just more than Mr. Floyd could take by virtue of those heart conditions.” On April 12 a cardiologist, Dr. Jonathan Rich, called the death “absolutely preventable.”

    Tr. Tobin · Star Tribune, day-by-day recap · Ct. App. opinion p. 5

  28. Apr 12, 2021Denied

    No sequestered jury after Brooklyn Center

    A police shooting in the suburb of Brooklyn Center sets off protests and unrest. The defense asks to sequester the jury, and the judge says no.

    The jurors were already partly sequestered, brought in and out of the building by deputies and kept apart from everyone else in it. The judge told them again to stay away from any news of the case.

    Ct. App. opinion pp. 9, 6–7 · Trial management order

  29. Apr 13 – 14, 2021

    The defense case: “justified,” and a heart that gave out

    A use-of-force expert, Barry Brodd, says Chauvin was justified. A retired medical examiner, Dr. David Fowler, says heart disease, drugs and other factors together caused the death.

    Brodd, a retired police officer, said Chauvin was “acting with objective reasonableness,” and didn’t count holding Floyd face down as force at all. Fowler, a former chief medical examiner of Maryland, said Floyd’s heart went into a fatal rhythm during the restraint and didn’t list a lack of oxygen as a cause.

    Shawanda Hill, who was in the car, said Floyd had fallen asleep before the police came. Another passenger, Morries Hall, had said he’d take the Fifth if called, and he wasn’t called.

    Tr. Brodd · Tr. Hill · Notice of Mar 31, 2021 · Star Tribune, day-by-day recap

  30. Apr 15, 2021

    He won’t testify

    With the jury out of the room, Chauvin tells the judge he’ll use his Fifth Amendment right. The defense rests.

    He told the judge it was his decision alone. The State then called Dr. Tobin back briefly in rebuttal, to answer a defense point about carbon monoxide.

    The jury was told it couldn’t hold his silence against him. A juror said in an interview after the verdict that the jurors had talked about it anyway, which became part of his appeal.

    AP, Apr 15, 2021 · Ct. App. opinion p. 10

  31. Apr 19, 2021

    “This wasn’t policing. This was murder.”

    Steve Schleicher closes for the State: “Believe your eyes.” Jerry Blackwell gets the last word. Floyd died, he says, “because Mr. Chauvin’s heart was too small.”

    Schleicher told the jury the case was “not called the State of Minnesota v. The Police,” and called it “a pro-police prosecution.” Of the claim that Floyd died of an overdose, he said, “That’s not common sense. That’s nonsense.”

    Tr. Closing, 01:42:51 · 14:10 · 43:05 · NPR, Apr 19, 2021

  32. Apr 19, 2021

    “The previous 16 minutes and 59 seconds”

    Eric Nelson closes: a reasonable officer would weigh the whole encounter, not just nine minutes and 29 seconds, and the State hasn’t proved what killed Floyd.

    “A reasonable police officer would in fact take into consideration the previous 16 minutes and 59 seconds,” he said. “Human behavior is unpredictable.” Most of his argument went to the reasonable officer standard and the struggle at the squad car.

    On the cause of death: “If a person has drugs in their system and that drug causes an overdose in the context of the police restraint, it’s not the natural consequence of the restraint. It’s the natural consequence of the deceased’s actions.”

    Tr. Defense closing, 54:53 · NPR, Apr 19, 2021

  33. Apr 19, 2021Denied

    No mistrial

    Before the jury goes out, Nelson asks for a mistrial over the publicity and a congresswoman’s words. The judge says no, and the jury begins to deliberate.

    Rep. Maxine Waters of California had said that if Chauvin were acquitted, protesters should “stay on the street” and “get more confrontational.”

    Ct. App. opinion p. 9

  34. Apr 20, 2021Guilty

    Guilty on all three counts

    The jury finds him guilty of second-degree murder, third-degree murder and second-degree manslaughter. It had agreed by 1:45 p.m. on its second day.

    Judge Cahill read the verdicts that afternoon and then asked each juror, by number, if they were theirs. All twelve said yes. The foreperson was Juror 19.

    The State asked for his bail to be revoked, and the judge did it on the spot and sent him into the sheriff’s custody.

    Verdict, Count I · Count II · Count III · Tr. Verdict

  35. Two sentences

    The state sentence, then a federal case of its own, over the same evening and one other.

  36. May 6, 2021

    Federal charges

    A federal grand jury’s indictment charges all four officers with violating Floyd’s constitutional rights. A second one charges Chauvin over a 2017 arrest of a 14-year-old boy.

    The federal crime is using the power of a police job, under color of law, to take away someone’s rights, here the right to be free from unreasonable force. A state and the federal government can each prosecute the same act, because they’re separate governments.

    In the 2017 case, by his own later admission, he hit the boy in the head with a flashlight, held him by the throat, and knelt on his neck and back for 15 to 16 minutes while he lay handcuffed and not resisting.

    Indictment, ECF 1 · Plea agreement pp. 6–7

  37. May 11, 2021

    Four reasons for a longer sentence

    Chauvin had given up a jury on this, so the judge decides: he abused his position of trust and authority, treated Floyd with particular cruelty, did it in front of children, and acted with three others.

    “It was particularly cruel to kill George Floyd slowly by preventing his ability to breathe when Mr. Floyd had already made it clear he was having trouble breathing,” the judge wrote.

    He found the State hadn’t proved a fifth reason it had argued, that Floyd was particularly vulnerable.

    Findings of May 11, 2021 pp. 3–5 · Sentencing order p. 4

  38. Jun 2, 2021

    The State asks for 30 years

    That’s double the top of the guidelines range for someone with no criminal record.

    The presumptive sentence for second-degree unintentional murder, with no record, is 150 months, inside a range of 128 to 180. The State asked for 360 months.

    State’s memorandum, Jun 2, 2021

  39. Jun 2, 2021

    He asks for probation

    His lawyer asks for time served and probation, saying that in his mind Chauvin was “simply performing his lawful duty.”

    The memo called what he did “an error made in good faith reliance” on his experience and training, “not intentional commission of an illegal act.” He had also asked for a new trial, on 11 grounds, from the publicity to a juror’s answers in jury selection.

    Defense memorandum, Jun 2, 2021 p. 11

  40. Jun 24, 2021Denied

    No new trial

    The judge turns down a new trial, and a hearing to question jurors about misconduct.

    He found Chauvin hadn’t shown that the court “abused its discretion or committed error such that Defendant was deprived of his constitutional right to a fair trial,” or any misconduct by the prosecutors or the jurors.

    Order of Jun 24, 2021

  41. Jun 25, 2021

    270 months

    Twenty-two and a half years, ten years past the presumptive sentence. He’s convicted of second-degree murder. The other two guilty verdicts are never entered, because they’re lesser offenses of it.

    The judge rested the sentence on two of the four reasons, the abuse of trust and the particular cruelty. A sentence can’t rest on “sympathy, bias, passion, or public opinion,” he wrote, and Chauvin “treated Mr. Floyd without respect and denied him the dignity owed to all human beings.”

    Before the sentence, Chauvin offered his condolences to Floyd’s family and said, “There’s going to be some other information in the future that would be of interest.” His mother spoke for him, and two of Floyd’s brothers spoke in court.

    Sentencing order pp. 1–2, 20–22 · NPR, Jun 25, 2021

  42. Dec 15, 2021

    “At this time guilty, Your Honor”

    In federal court in St. Paul, Chauvin pleads guilty to violating Floyd’s rights, and to the 2017 count.

    In his plea agreement he admitted that “no later than the time the officers decided not to apply the hobble,” his force “became objectively unreasonable and excessive,” and that he knew it was wrong “in part, because it was contrary to his training.” He agreed that his actions kept Floyd from getting enough oxygen to live.

    The deal called for 20 to 25 years, served at the same time as his state sentence, in federal prison. The 2017 count came in by information, and the separate 2017 indictment was dropped.

    Plea transcript p. 22 · Plea agreement pp. 1–6

  43. Feb 24, 2022

    The other three officers are convicted in federal court

    A federal jury finds Tou Thao, J. Alexander Kueng and Thomas Lane guilty of violating Floyd’s rights. Chauvin, having pleaded, wasn’t part of that trial.

    All three were convicted of depriving Floyd of medical care, and Thao and Kueng also of failing to stop Chauvin. Their cases are separate from this one.

    AP, Feb 24, 2022

  44. Jul 7, 2022

    Twenty-one years in federal prison

    Judge Paul Magnuson sentences him to 252 months, cut to 245, running at the same time as his state sentence.

    That’s 240 months for violating Floyd’s rights and 12 for the 2017 count, then five years of supervised release. The 245 months make up for time he spent in state custody that federal prison won’t count. The government had asked for 25 years.

    Judgment pp. 2–3 · Government memorandum, ECF 375

  45. The appeal

    Twelve reasons the verdict and the sentence were wrong, and three courts that wouldn’t change either.

  46. Apr 25, 2022

    His appeal: twelve reasons

    His new lawyer, William Mohrman, files his brief in the Court of Appeals. The biggest arguments are the publicity, a juror, and whether a police officer can commit felony murder at all.

    He argued the trial should have been moved or delayed, pointing to the riots, the settlement and a courthouse “surrounded by barbed wire and soldiers.”

    And he said the felony-murder law turns into “strict liability” for police, because officers are “duty bound to ‘assault’ suspects resisting arrest.”

    His appeal brief p. 57, 65

  47. Sept 7, 2022

    The State: “The truth matters”

    Its brief, written with the lawyer Neal Katyal, calls the appeal “an industrial kitchen-sink’s worth of arguments.”

    “George Floyd did not die because of a heart attack, drugs, carbon monoxide, or a tumor, as the defense alternatively tried to claim,” it said. “George Floyd died for a simple reason: because he could not breathe.”

    Chauvin “says the quiet part out loud,” it said, “and argues that police officers cannot ever be convicted of assault.”

    State’s appeal brief pp. 19–21

  48. Jan 18, 2023

    Argued in the Court of Appeals

    Before three judges, at 9:30 in the morning, and streamed live.

    The panel was Judges Reyes, Larson and Klaphake, the last a retired judge serving by appointment. Judge Reyes wrote the opinion.

    Ct. App. opinion p. 1 · Minnesota Judicial Branch

  49. Apr 17, 2023Affirmed

    Conviction and sentence affirmed

    All twelve arguments fail. A police officer who uses unreasonable force that amounts to assault can be convicted of felony murder, the court holds.

    “Police officers undoubtedly have a challenging, difficult, and sometimes dangerous job,” the court wrote. “However, no one is above the law.”

    On the publicity, it held that refusing to move or delay a trial is no mistake when a judge takes enough steps to make sure the jurors can be fair. On the sentence, it held that the particular cruelty alone justified going past the guidelines.

    Ct. App. opinion, Apr 17, 2023 pp. 1–2, 48–50

  50. Jul 18, 2023Denied

    The Minnesota Supreme Court won’t hear it

    It turns down his petition for review, ending his direct appeal in the state courts.

    Turning a case down isn’t a ruling on it. It leaves the Court of Appeals’ decision as the last word.

    Docket, No. 23-416

  51. Aug 15, 2023Dismissed

    His federal appeal is too late

    He’d filed it himself, a year after his federal sentence. The Eighth Circuit dismisses it as untimely.

    His plea deal had already given up his right to appeal anything but the court’s power over the case. His lawyer, Eric Nelson, withdrew from the federal case that September.

    Eighth Circuit, ECF 538 · Government response p. 3 · ECF 541

  52. Nov 20, 2023Denied

    The U.S. Supreme Court won’t hear it either

    It turns down his petition for certiorari. Minnesota hadn’t even filed a response.

    He asked the Court whether a community that had lived through “catastrophic violent riots” should be presumed biased against a police officer, and whether evidence after a trial that a juror hid his views has to be considered. The Minneapolis police officers’ union filed a brief supporting him.

    Petition p. 2 · Docket, No. 23-416

  53. Still fighting both

    Since 2023 he has gone back to both courts, with new lawyers and new theories. Nothing has been undone.

  54. Nov 13, 2023

    He asks to undo his federal guilty plea

    Filing on his own, he says his lawyer never told him a pathologist had written that a tumor, not Chauvin, caused Floyd’s death.

    The pathologist, Dr. William Schaetzel, had emailed Nelson, the judge’s chambers and a county prosecutor during the 2021 trial about the tumor the autopsy found. Chauvin says that if he’d known, he “would not have pled guilty.” His plea deal had given up this kind of motion except for ineffective assistance of counsel, which is the claim he makes.

    The government answered that the autopsy findings “came out at his state trial,” and that the same kind of expert testimony “was not persuasive to the jury.”

    Motion to vacate, ECF 544 · Schaetzel email p. 11 · Government response pp. 7, 11

  55. Nov 24, 2023

    Stabbed in federal prison

    Another prisoner stabs him 22 times at the federal prison in Tucson, Arizona. He survives, and the man is charged with attempted murder.

    The man told investigators he targeted Chauvin because of the case. In 2024 Chauvin was moved to a low-security federal prison in Big Spring, Texas.

    AP, Aug 20, 2024

  56. Nov 23, 2024

    His first petition in state court

    A post-conviction petition, the way to challenge a state conviction once the appeal is over.

    It centered on the tumor found at the autopsy, and on whether the medical examiner had been pushed toward calling the death a homicide. Chauvin later said he didn’t mean to argue those points there.

    Star Tribune, Dec 4, 2025

  57. Dec 15, 2024Granted

    His experts may examine Floyd’s heart tissue

    In the federal case, Judge Magnuson lets the defense examine tissue samples, slides, fluids and photographs from the autopsy.

    The government asked him to reconsider. He refused, reminding it that “this matter is separate from Defendant’s state prosecution.” In January 2025 he put the hearing off for up to 90 days while the testing went on, and asked for a report every 30 days.

    Nothing has been filed on the public docket since. No result of the testing has been made public, and the motion hasn’t been decided.

? Terms

Plain English

Words these pages use because the courts use them. Two courts convicted him, a state court and a federal one, and a few words belong to only one of them.

The courts

Hennepin County District Court
The state trial court for Minneapolis and the county around it. Judge Peter Cahill tried the case there. A different judge, Paul Scoggin, has heard his petitions since.
Minnesota Court of Appeals
The state court that hears appeals from trial courts. Three of its judges decide each case.
Minnesota Supreme Court
The state’s highest court. It chooses which appeals to hear, and it turned down his.
Federal court
The United States’ own courts, separate from the state’s. The federal case was in the U.S. District Court in St. Paul, before Judge Paul Magnuson.
Attorney General
Minnesota’s chief lawyer, elected statewide. Keith Ellison’s office took over the prosecution from the county prosecutor in May 2020.
Two prosecutions
A state and the federal government count as separate governments, so each can prosecute the same act under its own laws. That isn’t double jeopardy.

The paperwork

Complaint
The document that starts a criminal case in Minnesota. It lists the charges and the facts the prosecutor says support them. An amended complaint replaces it.
Grand jury
A group of citizens who hear the prosecutor’s evidence in private and decide whether to charge someone. Minnesota only requires one for crimes that carry life in prison, like first-degree murder, so this case started with a complaint instead.
Indictment
A charge brought by a grand jury. The federal case started with one.
Information
A charge a prosecutor files without a grand jury. In federal court that takes the defendant’s consent, which he gave for the 2017 count.
Docket
The court’s numbered list of everything filed in a case. Entry 42 is the forty-second thing filed.
Plea agreement
A written deal between a defendant and the prosecutors. He pleads guilty to some charges, and they agree on others to drop and on a range of sentence.
Broadcast transcript
A word-for-word record. The court reporter’s transcript of this trial was never posted, so these pages quote transcripts typed from the court’s own video feed and cite them by its clock.

The charges

Second-degree unintentional murder
Causing a death, without meaning to, while committing a felony. Here the felony was third-degree assault. This is the one count he was convicted and sentenced on.
Third-degree assault
Intentionally using unlawful force on someone, without their consent, that causes substantial bodily harm. He only had to mean to use the force, not to cause that much harm.
Third-degree murder
Causing a death by an act “eminently dangerous” to others, done with “a depraved mind,” without meaning to kill. Also called depraved-mind murder.
Second-degree manslaughter
Causing a death by culpable negligence, which means consciously taking a chance of causing death or great harm that an ordinary careful person would see.
Lesser offense
A crime contained in a more serious one. Minnesota law allows only one conviction for the same act, so the other two guilty verdicts were never entered as convictions.
Under color of law
Acting with the power of a government job, like a police officer on duty. The federal civil rights law makes it a crime to use that power to take away someone’s constitutional rights.
Aiding and abetting
Intentionally helping someone else commit a crime. The other three officers were charged that way.

How a case moves

Probable cause
Enough evidence to make it reasonable to believe a person committed the crime. It’s the bar a charge has to clear to go to trial, and it’s a lot lower than proof.
Venue
Where a trial is held. A defendant can ask to move it if publicity would make a fair jury impossible where the crime happened.
Continuance
Putting a trial or a hearing off to a later date.
Severance
Splitting one trial of several defendants into separate trials.
Sequester
Keeping jurors away from news and the public, sometimes in a hotel, so nothing outside the courtroom reaches them.
Jury selection
Questioning possible jurors one by one to find out if they can be fair. Also called voir dire.
Peremptory strike
Removing a possible juror without giving a reason. Each side gets a set number. Here the judge raised his to 18 and the State’s to 10.
Excused for cause
Removing a possible juror for a specific reason they can’t be fair, like having already made up their mind. There’s no limit on these.
Alternate juror
A juror who hears the whole trial but only deliberates if one of the twelve has to leave.
Anonymous jury
Jurors known in court only by number, their names kept from the public until the judge releases them.
Earlier incident
Evidence of something that happened before the case, which a jury may only use for a narrow purpose the judge sets. Minnesota calls it Spreigl evidence.
The Fifth
The right not to be a witness against yourself. A defendant who uses it doesn’t testify, and the jury is told it can’t hold that against him.
Rebuttal
Evidence or argument that answers the other side’s. The State goes last because it has to prove the case.
Beyond a reasonable doubt
The level of proof needed to convict. It means proof that leaves the jury firmly convinced, not proof beyond every possible doubt.
Unanimous
All twelve jurors agreeing. A verdict in a criminal case has to be.
Polling the jury
Asking each juror out loud, one at a time, if the verdict read is theirs.

What the jury had to decide

Substantial causal factor
The test for causing a death. His acts didn’t have to be the only cause, only a substantial one. Other causes don’t excuse him unless one of them alone caused the death.
Superseding cause
Something that comes afterward and breaks the chain, so that it alone causes the death. It’s the one way another cause can clear a defendant.
Authorized use of force
The defense the jury was told about. No crime is committed if a police officer uses reasonable force to make a lawful arrest, and the State had to prove his force wasn’t that.
Reasonable officer
How force is judged. The question is what a reasonable officer in the same situation would have done at the moment the force was used, not what the officer meant or what hindsight shows.
Prone restraint
Holding a person face down. Police training warns it can make it hard to breathe, and says to move the person onto their side once they’re under control.
Recovery position
Rolling a person onto their side so they can breathe more easily. The Minneapolis police’s own training said to do it.
Hobble
A strap that ties a person’s legs together. The officers talked about using one and didn’t.
Maximal restraint technique
The Minneapolis police method of strapping a struggling person’s legs and moving them off their stomach. The defense said what the officers did began as one. The State said the knee was no part of it.
Asphyxia
Death from not getting enough oxygen. The State’s doctors said the restraint caused it.
Paraganglioma
A rare tumor that can release adrenaline. One was found at the autopsy and called incidental, meaning the medical examiner didn’t think it played a part.

The sentence

Sentencing guidelines
Minnesota’s chart of how long a sentence should be for each crime, set by a state commission. A judge has to stay inside its range unless there are substantial and compelling reasons not to.
Presumptive sentence
What the guidelines call for. For this crime and no past record it’s 150 months, inside a range of 128 to 180.
Aggravating factor
A fact that makes a crime worse than the usual one of its kind, like particular cruelty. It has to be proved before a judge can go past the guidelines.
Blakely
A 2004 U.S. Supreme Court ruling that a jury has to find the facts behind a longer sentence, unless the defendant gives that up. He gave it up, so the judge decided them.
Upward departure
A sentence longer than the guidelines’ range. His was 270 months against a top of 180.
Concurrent
Served at the same time. His state and federal sentences run together, not one after the other.
Supervised release
A period after a federal prison term when a person lives under conditions the court sets, checked by a probation officer.

After the verdict

Motion for a new trial
Asking the trial judge to throw out the verdict and start over because something went wrong. It comes before any appeal.
Schwartz hearing
A hearing in Minnesota where a judge questions jurors about possible misconduct after a trial. He asked for one and was refused.
Direct appeal
The first appeal after a conviction, which looks only at what happened at trial. His was decided in 2023.
Petition for review
Asking the Minnesota Supreme Court to hear a case after the Court of Appeals has ruled. It turns most of them down.
Certiorari
Asking the U.S. Supreme Court to hear a case. It takes very few, and turning one down decides nothing about the case itself.
Post-conviction petition
A way to challenge a state conviction after the appeal is over, usually with something that wasn’t in the trial record. It goes back to the trial court first.
Knaffla rule
Minnesota’s rule that a petition after the appeal can’t raise what was raised, or could have been raised, on appeal, with narrow exceptions.
Structural error
A mistake in how a case was run that’s so basic a court doesn’t ask whether it changed the result. His lawyer says the way he was charged is one.
Motion to vacate
The federal version of a post-conviction petition, under section 2255 of the federal code. His asks to undo his federal guilty plea.
Ineffective assistance
A lawyer’s work falling so far short that it denied the client a fair process. It’s the one kind of claim his plea deal let him bring later.
Stay
Pausing a case, or a ruling, until something else is decided.

The stamp on a ruling

Granted
The court said yes to the request on this card, whichever side made it.
Granted in part
The court gave some of what was asked for on this card and said no to the rest.
Denied
The court said no to the request on this card.
Reinstated
A charge that had been dismissed was put back, so the jury could decide it.
Guilty
The jury’s verdict, or his own plea in court. It becomes a conviction once the court enters judgment on it.
Affirmed
The court above agreed with the court below and left the conviction and the sentence as they were.
Dismissed
Thrown out without a ruling on whether the claims in it were right.
