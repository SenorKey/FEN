TikTok v. Garland

TLDR Case Flow

Where it standsThe law was upheld in January 2025, and it hasn’t been enforced. TikTok’s US business was sold to a new company in January 2026, and a new case in the D.C. Circuit says the sale breaks the law. The court hasn’t decided yet whether it can even hear that case.

The case

TikTok, ByteDance and the creators against the Attorney General, in the D.C. Circuit and then the Supreme Court. Did the law break the Constitution?

  1. The first fight · Speech, or just ownership?

    Before any test can apply

    Does the First Amendment even apply to this law?

    Assumed so · Jan 17, 2025

    This is where the government started. The law doesn’t ban any video or tell anyone what to say, it argued. It only says who can own TikTok, and ByteDance, a foreign company abroad, has no free-speech rights to begin with. If that’s right, there’s no First Amendment question at all, and the law just has to be reasonable.

    TikTok and the creators said the law shuts down an American company’s speech and 170 million people’s place to post, so of course it applies. The D.C. Circuit agreed with them on this. The Supreme Court didn’t decide it. It assumed the First Amendment applies and moved on, which basically means the law passes even if TikTok is right about this part. Justice Sotomayor would have said yes outright.

    What each side saidThe government: a foreign company can’t use “an American puppet as a mouthpiece.” TikTok: “It’s a burden on TikTok’s speech, so the First Amendment applies.”

    If no

    Outcome

    No free-speech case

    Then

    The law just has to be reasonable

    That’s the outcome the government asked for first. A law about who owns a company, with no First Amendment question in it, would almost certainly stand.

    Neither court went this way. The D.C. Circuit said the First Amendment applies, and the Supreme Court left the question open.

    If yes

  2. Which test? · The hardest one or the middle one

    What the test turns on

    Is the law aimed at what people say?

    No · content-neutral, Jan 17, 2025

    Courts test speech laws harder or easier depending on what they’re aimed at. A law aimed at what people say, its topic or its message, is content-based, and it gets strict scrutiny, the hardest test, which most laws fail. A law that applies whatever is said is content-neutral, and it only gets the middle test, intermediate scrutiny.

    So this is the fight over which test applies, and the two cards inside it are the two ways TikTok said the law is aimed at speech. Either one would have been enough. The case goes on to the test either way, but the answer here decides how hard that test is.

    A yes on either one is enough

    Part one · The words of the law

    Does the law pick out apps by what’s on them?

    No · not as applied, Jan 17, 2025

    The law’s general rule only covers apps where people post and share their own content, and it leaves out apps whose main purpose is reviews of products, businesses or travel. TikTok said that makes the whole law content-based from the start.

    The Supreme Court said that part doesn’t matter here. The review-site exclusion only applies to the law’s general track, and TikTok was named in the law itself. Looking only at TikTok’s part, the law is about who controls the app, not what’s on it.

    or

    Part two · The reason for the law

    Is the law’s reason about content?

    No · the data reason isn’t, Jan 17, 2025

    The government gave two reasons. One is data, that China could collect information on 170 million Americans. The other is covert content manipulation, that China could secretly push ByteDance to change what Americans see. TikTok said the second one is about content, plain and simple.

    The Court called the data reason “decidedly content agnostic.” It didn’t decide whether the second one is about content, because it found Congress would have passed the law for the data reason alone. Justice Gorsuch wrote that he had “serious reservations” about calling the law content-neutral, but he agreed with the result.

    Either way

  3. The test, part one · The reason

    Why the government wanted it

    Is the government’s reason important enough?

    Yes · the data reason alone, Jan 17, 2025

    Under the middle test the government needs an important reason, and under the hardest one a compelling one. Here that means its two reasons, data and covert manipulation. If either one is strong enough, the law gets past this card.

    TikTok and the creators said a government can’t act because of what people might be persuaded of, so the manipulation reason doesn’t count at all. On data they mostly argued that this law doesn’t fit it, which is the next card. The Supreme Court decided this card on data alone.

    A yes on either one is enough

    Reason one · Data

    Is keeping Americans’ data away from China a strong enough reason?

    Yes · Jan 17, 2025

    The government said TikTok collects huge amounts of personal data, on its users and on people in their contacts who never signed up, and that China’s laws can make ByteDance hand it over. Its lawyer pointed to China’s past hacks of federal personnel files, Equifax and Anthem.

    “Petitioners do not dispute” that this interest is important, the Court wrote. “Nor could they.” Even the creators’ lawyer said at argument that data security “is compelling.” The D.C. Circuit called it compelling too.

    or

    Reason two · What Americans see

    Can the government act to stop China secretly steering what Americans see?

    Not decided

    This is the reason TikTok said is forbidden. The creators’ lawyer put it this way at argument: restricting speech “because it might sow doubt about our leaders or undermine democracy” is what “our enemies do.” The government said it isn’t about any message, it’s about a foreign adversary having that power at all.

    The D.C. Circuit said this reason was compelling too. The Supreme Court didn’t decide it. Justice Gorsuch wrote that “One man’s ‘covert content manipulation’ is another’s ‘editorial discretion,’” and said the Court was right not to rest on it.

    If no

    Outcome

    The law falls

    Then

    Its reason wasn’t enough

    If neither reason were strong enough, the part of the law that names TikTok couldn’t be enforced, and ByteDance could keep TikTok.

    That’s where TikTok wanted the case to end. It didn’t, because the data reason alone was enough for every judge and justice.

    If yes

  4. The test, part two · The fit

    Whether a smaller fix would do

    Is a forced sale more than the problem needs?

    No · Jan 17, 2025

    Even with a good reason, a law can’t burden a lot more speech than it needs to. That’s tailoring. TikTok offered smaller fixes, each one, it said, a less restrictive alternative: the 90-page security agreement it had negotiated, with Oracle checking the code, a ban on sharing users’ data with anyone, or a warning label on the app.

    The government said none of them would work, because it couldn’t trust ByteDance or watch it closely enough. The Court sided with the government. The law isn’t an outright ban, it said, but “a conditional ban,” lifted the moment China’s control ends, and the alternatives “do not alter our tailoring analysis.” It gave deference to Congress’s judgment about national security.

    If yes

    Outcome

    The law falls

    Then

    A smaller fix would have done

    If a smaller fix would have worked as well, the forced sale would fail the test, and the part of the law that names TikTok couldn’t be enforced.

    The Court didn’t go there. It said Congress could reasonably decide that only a sale would do.

    If no

  5. Only the appeals court answered these · Dec 6, 2024

    TikTok’s other three claims

    Does the law break the Constitution any other way?

    No, all three · Dec 6, 2024

    TikTok also said the law breaks three other parts of the Constitution. The D.C. Circuit turned down all three. The Supreme Court only agreed to answer the free-speech question, so these were decided once, by the appeals court, and that’s where they stand.

    Any one of them would have been enough to stop the law, so a yes on any card inside this one would have meant TikTok won.

    A yes on any one is enough

    Claim one · Equal treatment

    Did Congress single TikTok out unfairly?

    No · Dec 6, 2024

    TikTok said Congress treated it worse than every other app without a good enough reason, which would break the Constitution’s promise of equal protection.

    The court said Congress had its reasons. “The Act singled out TikTok because of its known characteristics and history.”

    or

    Claim two · Punishment

    Is the law a punishment without a trial?

    No · Dec 6, 2024

    The Constitution bars Congress from passing a bill of attainder, a law that punishes a named person or group without a trial. TikTok said a law naming it is exactly that.

    The court said the law asks for “a sale, not a confiscation,” and leaves the way out open forever. The government’s effort, it wrote, “reflects a forward-looking prophylactic, not a backward-looking punitive, purpose.”

    or

    Claim three · Property

    Does the law take TikTok’s property without paying for it?

    No · Dec 6, 2024

    The government can take private property only if it pays fairly for it. TikTok said forcing a sale, maybe at a fire-sale price, is a taking.

    The court said the problem was China. Its export rules were what made a sale hard, and “the PRC, not the divestiture offramp in the Act, is the source of TikTok’s difficulty.”

    If yes

    Outcome

    The law falls

    Then

    On other grounds

    A win on any of these would have stopped the part of the law that names TikTok, whatever the free-speech answer was.

    None got that far, and the Supreme Court never looked at them.

    If no

    Outcome

    The law stands

    Then

    ByteDance has until January 19, 2025

    This is where the case ended. Every judge and justice agreed the law could be enforced. ByteDance had until January 19, 2025 to sell, or app stores and web hosts in the US would have to drop TikTok.

    What happened next wasn’t up to a court. It’s the chain below.

After the ruling

What happened to the law once it was upheld, and the new case that says it’s being broken, Tan v. Trump, in the D.C. Circuit. The challengers there are other people.

  1. January 19, 2025 · The deadline passes

    Not a question for a court

    Was the law enforced?

    No · Jan 20, 2025 onward

    This one wasn’t decided by a court. Only the Attorney General can enforce this law, and it took effect the day before President Trump took office. TikTok shut itself off for about half a day, then came back on his promise.

    On his first day he ordered the Attorney General not to enforce it, and he renewed that order four times. The Justice Department wrote to Apple, Google and the rest promising them no liability. That was non-enforcement, not the law’s own extension, and it’s part of what Tan v. Trump challenges.

    If yes

    Outcome

    Cut off

    Then

    App stores and web hosts drop TikTok

    That’s what the law said would happen on January 19, 2025 unless TikTok was sold first. Any company that kept offering it would face fines of up to $5,000 for each US user.

    It’s roughly what the challengers in Tan v. Trump say the law required then, though they aren’t asking for a ban now.

    If no

  2. Tan v. Trump · Filed March 5, 2026

    The case is here

    The case after the case

    Does the 2026 sale break the law?

    Undecided

    The law only lifts the ban after a qualified divestiture, a sale that leaves no operational relationship with ByteDance. President Trump decided on September 25, 2025 that this deal is one, and the new company took over on January 22, 2026. Two software engineers, later joined by a former ByteDance employee, say it isn’t, and they’ve asked the D.C. Circuit to say so.

    Before the court gets to the deal itself, it has two questions about the challengers, and the government says they lose on both. The challengers need a yes on all three cards inside this one. The court hasn’t ruled on any of them. On July 20, 2026 it sent the first two on to the judges who will decide the whole case.

    A yes to all three is needed

    Part one · The deadline

    Did they sue in time?

    Undecided

    The law gives 90 days to challenge a government action, a deadline to sue. The government says the last action was the President’s order of September 25, 2025, so the March 2026 petition “missed by several months.”

    The challengers say that order only approved the deal “once its implementation agreements are executed,” so nothing was final until the sale closed on January 22, and they sued “a few weeks later.” The government says the only thing that happened in January was the President’s post about it, which isn’t an order.

    and

    Part two · Standing

    Can these challengers sue at all?

    Undecided

    To bring a case you need standing, a real harm of your own. The challengers say Reid uses TikTok and his data is still shared with ByteDance, that Doe lost his job in the deal, and that Tan and Reid lost money as shareholders in TikTok’s rivals.

    The government says shareholders can’t sue over harm to their companies, that the effect on stock prices is “highly speculative,” and that the data claim is “speculation piled upon speculation.”

    and

    Part three · The deal

    Does ByteDance keep an “operational relationship”?

    Undecided

    This is the heart of it. The challengers say ByteDance still owns the algorithm and licenses it to the new company, still runs its e-commerce, advertising and marketing, and that TikTok’s global chief executive sits on its board. They say ByteDance staff still get US user data. Those are their claims, and none has been tested.

    The government hasn’t argued this part yet. Its view so far is the President’s own findings, that the new company controls the algorithms, the code and moderation, keeps US data in an American company’s cloud, and has the algorithm retrained on US data.

    If yes

    Outcome

    Declared unlawful

    Then

    The Attorney General has to investigate

    That’s what the challengers ask for, a ruling that the extensions, the failure to investigate and the sale broke the law, and an order for the Attorney General to investigate. They don’t ask the court to ban TikTok.

    What would follow isn’t clear. The law leaves enforcement, and its fines, to the Attorney General.

    If no

    Outcome

    The sale stands

    Then

    TikTok stays with the new company

    If the challengers sued too late or can’t sue at all, the case ends without any court ruling on the deal itself. If the court does rule on the deal and finds it meets the law, the sale is settled.

    Either way, the new company keeps running TikTok in the US, as it has since January 2026.

The case after the case

The Supreme Court’s case is over. It sent its judgment down on February 18, 2025, and the D.C. Circuit closed the case ten days later. What happened to the law after that, the orders not to enforce it, the sale and the new company, happened outside both courts, and these pages say so card by card.

Then in March 2026 a new case was filed under the same law, Tan v. Trump, No. 26-1047 in the D.C. Circuit. Two software engineers who own stock in Alphabet and Meta brought it, and later a former employee of a ByteDance company, against President Trump and the Attorney General. It says the sale isn’t the one the law demands. It’s still open. These pages show it as far as its record goes, with what each side says marked as what that side says.

? Terms

Plain English

Words these pages use because the courts use them. The case went through two courts, the D.C. Circuit and the Supreme Court, and a few words only matter after the ruling, in the case that came after it.

The courts

D.C. Circuit
The U.S. Court of Appeals for the District of Columbia Circuit, in Washington. The law sends every challenge to it first, and to no other court.
Panel
The three judges of an appeals court who hear a case or a motion. The D.C. Circuit has about a dozen judges, and different panels handle different steps.
Merits panel
The panel that will decide a case itself, as opposed to the ones that handle motions along the way.
Attorney General
The head of the Justice Department. The law gives this office alone the job of enforcing it, which is why the case is named after one.
Solicitor General
The lawyer who argues the federal government’s cases in the Supreme Court. Elizabeth Prelogar held the job when this case was argued.
Per curiam
Latin for “by the court.” An opinion that no one justice signs, issued in the name of the whole Court.
Concurring opinion
A justice who agrees with the result writing separately to give other or extra reasons. Concurring “in the judgment” means agreeing with the result but not the Court’s reasoning.

The paperwork

Docket
The court’s numbered list of everything filed in a case. Entry 42 is the forty-second thing filed.
Petition for review
How a case under this law starts: a request made straight to the appeals court, with no trial court first.
Exclusive jurisdiction
Being the only court allowed to hear a kind of case. The law gave that to the D.C. Circuit.
Consolidated
Separate cases joined so they’re briefed, argued and decided together.
Brief
A side’s written argument to the court.
Amicus brief
A brief from someone who isn’t a party, a “friend of the court,” giving the court a view of the case.
Declaration
A written statement a witness signs under penalty of perjury. There was no trial here, so the evidence was mostly declarations.
Record
Everything a court has in front of it to decide a case: the filings, the declarations, the evidence.
Classified evidence
Material the government keeps secret for national security. It filed some here for the judges alone, which the other side never got to see or answer.
Special master
Someone a court appoints to help with a task. TikTok asked for one with a security clearance to review the secret evidence.

How the case moved

Application
A request to the Supreme Court for emergency help before it has taken a case. Here, to pause the law before it took effect.
Injunction
A court order stopping something. One “pending review” pauses a law while a higher court looks at the case.
Certiorari
The Supreme Court agreeing to hear a case. Here it treated TikTok’s emergency applications as requests for it and granted them two days later.
Question presented
The exact question the Supreme Court agrees to answer. Here there was one: does the law, as applied to TikTok and the creators, violate the First Amendment.
As applied
A challenge to how a law works on the people bringing it, not to every possible use of it. The Supreme Court looked only at the part of the law that names TikTok.
Oral argument
The time the lawyers spend answering the judges’ questions in court. No witnesses, no new evidence and no jury.
Divided argument
Two lawyers splitting one side’s time. TikTok’s lawyer and the creators’ lawyer shared the challengers’ side.
Rebuttal
The few minutes the side that brought the case gets at the end, to answer the other side.
Mandate
The formal order that closes an appeal and makes the decision final in the court below.
Moot
No longer a live question, so there’s nothing left for a court to decide.

The law

Divestiture
Selling off a business, or enough of it that the seller no longer controls it.
Qualified divestiture
A sale the President finds ends a foreign adversary’s control and leaves no “operational relationship” with ByteDance. Only that lifts the law’s ban.
Operational relationship
The tie the law says can’t survive the sale, “including any cooperation with respect to the operation of a content recommendation algorithm or an agreement with respect to data sharing.”
Foreign adversary
A country the law treats as one: China, Russia, Iran and North Korea.
App stores and hosting
What the law actually forbids, which is offering the app in an app store or hosting it. So it binds companies like Apple, Google and Oracle, not TikTok’s users.
Civil penalty
A fine, not a crime. The law sets it at up to $5,000 for each US user reached through a violation.
The one extension
The law let the President add up to 90 days, once, if he told Congress a sale was in sight and showed binding agreements. No President used it.
The general track
The law’s second route, which lets the President name other apps controlled by a foreign adversary later. TikTok was named in the law itself.
Recommendation algorithm
The software that picks which video each user sees next on the “For You” feed. ByteDance owns it, and it’s developed and maintained in China.
Executive order
A written order from the President to the executive branch, published in the Federal Register.
Non-enforcement
The executive branch choosing not to bring cases under a law, here on the President’s orders. The law itself stays on the books.
CFIUS
The Committee on Foreign Investment in the United States, the federal panel that reviews foreign purchases of US businesses for security risks.
National security agreement
A contract setting the security conditions a company operates under. TikTok’s draft ran about 90 pages and was never signed.
Project Texas
TikTok’s plan to keep US users’ data in the United States, stored with Oracle and run by a separate US company. TikTok says it spent more than $2 billion on it.
IEEPA
The International Emergency Economic Powers Act, the 1977 law President Trump used for his 2020 ban. Courts found it couldn’t reach this kind of communication.

The free-speech test

First Amendment
The part of the Constitution that bars the government from “abridging the freedom of speech.” Courts check laws that burden speech with one of a few tests.
Assume without deciding
A court treating something as true for the sake of argument, because the answer wouldn’t change the result, and leaving the real question open.
Heightened scrutiny
Any of the stricter tests a court uses when a law burdens speech, instead of just asking whether the law is reasonable.
Strict scrutiny
The hardest test. The government needs a compelling reason and the narrowest way of getting there. Most laws that get it fail.
Intermediate scrutiny
The middle test. The government needs an important reason that isn’t about suppressing speech, and a law that doesn’t burden much more speech than it has to.
Content-based
Aimed at what is said, its topic, idea or message. A content-based law gets strict scrutiny.
Content-neutral
Applying whatever is said, like a limit on how loud a speaker can be. A content-neutral law gets intermediate scrutiny.
Singling out a speaker
A law aimed at one speaker. Courts look harder when that’s a way of getting at what the speaker says.
Tailoring
Whether a law fits its reason, covering what the problem needs and not much more.
Less restrictive alternative
Another way of meeting the same goal that burdens less speech. If one would work as well, the law can fail.
Underinclusive
Covering too little of the problem a law claims to fix, which can suggest the stated reason isn’t the real one.
Deference
A court giving weight to another branch’s judgment, here Congress’s predictions about national security.
Covert content manipulation
The government’s second reason: that China could secretly have ByteDance change what Americans see on TikTok. The Supreme Court didn’t rest on it.

The other claims

Equal protection
The Constitution’s promise that the government won’t treat people differently without a good enough reason.
Bill of attainder
A law that punishes a named person or group without a trial. The Constitution forbids Congress to pass one.
Taking
The government seizing private property, which the Constitution allows only with fair payment.

The case after the case

Standing
The right to bring a case at all. A challenger has to show a real injury of their own, caused by what they’re challenging, that a court could fix.
Deadline to sue
The time limit for bringing a case, also called a statute of limitations. This law allows 90 days after the government action being challenged.
Motion to dismiss
Asking a court to end a case without deciding who is right, for a reason like filing too late or having no standing.
Ripe
Ready for a court. A challenge to something that hasn’t happened yet comes too early.
Pseudonym
A made-up name, here “John Doe,” that a court lets a party use to keep their identity off the public record.
Certified index
The government’s official list of what’s in the record behind the decision being challenged. Briefing usually follows it.
Motion to govern
The parties’ proposal to the court for how a case should go on from here.
FOIA
The Freedom of Information Act, which lets anyone ask a federal agency for its records, and sue if it refuses.

The stamp on a ruling

Granted
The court said yes to the request on this card, whichever side made it.
Denied
The court said no to the request on this card.
Affirmed
The court above agreed with the court below and left its ruling as it was.
Referred
The court didn’t decide the request yet. It passed it on to the judges who will decide the whole case.
