U.S. Supreme Court · No. 24‑656  ·  D.C. Circuit · No. 24‑1113

TikTok v. Garland

TLDR View Chronological View Courtroom View Side by Side View

Side by Side Case Argumentation

Challengers: TikTok, ByteDance and the creators Government: the Attorney General

1Is this about speech at all, or only about who owns TikTok?

Challengers

Speech. TikTok Inc. is an American company, and choosing which videos to show is its own expression. The law shuts it down unless ByteDance sells, and it cuts 170 million Americans off from a place to speak. “It’s a burden on TikTok’s speech, so the First Amendment applies.”

Government

Ownership. The law doesn’t restrict any speech. It stops a foreign adversary from controlling a platform. ByteDance has no free-speech rights abroad, and “a foreign adversary abroad cannot manufacture First Amendment rights for itself by the simple expedient of using an American puppet as a mouthpiece.”

CourtNot decided. The Supreme Court assumed, without deciding, that the First Amendment applies. The D.C. Circuit had said it does, and Justice Sotomayor would have said so too.

Tr. p. 4 · TikTok’s brief pp. 20–25 · Government’s brief pp. 16, 21 · Opinion p. 9

Detailthe argument, what it rests on, where it is weak

Challengers

The argument

Picking and arranging other people’s speech is protected expression, the way a newspaper’s choices are, and TikTok Inc. makes those choices for its US feed. A law that bars it from doing so unless its owner sells is a heavy burden on that speech. The creators add their own rights, to speak to their audiences and to work with the publisher they choose.

Rests on

Moody v. NetChoice (2024), on platforms’ editorial choices. The government’s own concession in the D.C. Circuit that curating content on TikTok is a form of speech. The creators’ declarations about what they’d lose.

TikTok’s brief pp. 20–24 · Creators’ brief pp. 19–24

Authority

The First Amendment. NetChoice. Citizens United, on corporations’ speech rights.

Where it is weak

The law doesn’t forbid any video, and the creators could post the same things on a TikTok owned by someone else. Justice Barrett put it this way: “The law doesn’t say TikTok has to shut down. It says ByteDance has to divest.”

Tr. p. 59 · Opinion p. 7

Government

The argument

The law regulates who controls a company, which is conduct, not speech. ByteDance is a foreign company operating abroad and has no First Amendment rights. Its US subsidiary has no right to be controlled by a foreign adversary, or to use that adversary’s algorithm, and users have no right to post on a platform a foreign adversary controls.

Rests on

Agency for International Development v. Alliance for Open Society (2020), that foreign organizations abroad have no First Amendment rights. The long tradition of limits on foreign ownership of broadcasters and other communications.

Government’s brief pp. 19–23

Authority

Alliance for Open Society. Arcara v. Cloud Books (1986), on laws aimed at conduct that only happen to burden speech.

Where it is weak

The D.C. Circuit rejected this, finding TikTok Inc. “a domestic entity operating domestically.” The Supreme Court wouldn’t adopt it either, and said an effective ban “certainly burdens” users’ expression “in a non-trivial way.”

D.C. Cir. opinion p. 27 · Opinion p. 8

Logic chainhow the reasoning runs, and where it breaks
  1. Test

    Does the law burden the speech of anyone the First Amendment protects?

  2. Challengers

    An American company and American creators lose their platform.

  3. The burden

    The law shuts a speech platform unless it’s sold.

    The burden

    The law only changes who owns it, and every video can stay.

  4. Government

    Only a foreign company’s control is regulated, and it has no rights here.

  5. Where it lands

    Left open. The Court assumed the First Amendment applies, and upheld the law anyway.

2Is the law aimed at what people say?

Challengers

Yes. Its general rule only covers apps for user content and leaves out review sites, and Congress picked out TikTok partly because of what China might put in front of Americans. That makes it content-based and aimed at one speaker, so the hardest test applies.

Government

No. The law is about foreign control, and it applies whatever is on TikTok. Stopping data collection has nothing to do with content, and stopping covert manipulation is about any manipulation, whatever its message. So at most the middle test applies.

CourtContent-neutral, as applied to TikTok, so only intermediate scrutiny. Justice Gorsuch had “serious reservations” about that but agreed with the result.

TikTok’s brief pp. 25–27 · Government’s brief pp. 23–28 · Opinion pp. 10–13 · Gorsuch, J. p. 2

Detailthe argument, what it rests on, where it is weak

Challengers

The argument

The law starts from content, covering platforms where users share text, images and video and leaving out ones for business, product and travel reviews. It then singles out a single speaker, TikTok, for uniquely harsh treatment. And the government admits Congress acted partly over fears China could change “the mix of content” on it. Any of those makes it content-based or speaker-based, which calls for strict scrutiny.

Rests on

The text of the law’s definitions. The House report and what members of Congress said about TikTok’s content. The government’s own description of its manipulation concern.

TikTok’s brief pp. 25–31 · Tr. pp. 4–5

Authority

Reed v. Town of Gilbert (2015), on content-based laws. Citizens United, on laws aimed at one speaker.

Where it is weak

The review-site exclusion is in the general part of the law, not the part that names TikTok. And if Congress would have passed the law for data alone, its other motives may not change the test.

Opinion pp. 10–11, 18

Government

The argument

Nothing in the part of the law that names TikTok turns on what’s posted. The data interest “does not involve speech at all,” and the manipulation interest “seeks to prevent all such manipulation regardless of the content or viewpoint being advanced.” A law like that is content-neutral, and only gets intermediate scrutiny.

Rests on

The text of the law. Turner Broadcasting, on laws aimed at one kind of speaker for reasons unrelated to content.

Government’s brief pp. 16–17, 23–28

Authority

Turner Broadcasting System v. FCC (1994 and 1997). Ward v. Rock Against Racism (1989).

Where it is weak

Its manipulation reason is hard to separate from content. Justice Gorsuch doubted the law was content-neutral at all, and the D.C. Circuit said that reason “does … reference the content of TikTok’s speech.”

Gorsuch, J. p. 2 · D.C. Cir. opinion p. 30

Logic chainhow the reasoning runs, and where it breaks
  1. Test

    Does the law, as applied to TikTok, turn on what’s said, or on something else?

  2. Challengers

    Its coverage and its motive are both about content.

  3. The reason

    Fear of what China might show Americans is about content.

    The reason

    Fear of China collecting data isn’t about content at all.

  4. Government

    It applies whatever is on TikTok, so only the middle test applies.

  5. Where it lands

    Content-neutral, the Supreme Court held, resting on the data reason.

3Is China getting TikTok users’ data a real danger?

Challengers

Data security matters, and the creators’ lawyer called it “compelling.” But this law doesn’t really serve it. It covers apps by content and leaves out others that collect the same data, and China has easier ways to get Americans’ information.

Government

Yes, a grave one. TikTok collects vast data on 170 million users and their contacts, and Chinese law can make ByteDance hand it over. China has stolen Americans’ data before, from federal personnel files, Equifax and Anthem.

CourtYes. The data interest is important, and the Court found it enough on its own to sustain the law. It gave “substantial deference” to Congress’s judgment.

Tr. p. 81 · TikTok’s brief pp. 41–44 · Government’s brief pp. 29–37 · Opinion pp. 13–15

Detailthe argument, what it rests on, where it is weak

Challengers

The argument

If data were really the worry, Congress wouldn’t have drawn the law around user-generated content and left out e-commerce apps that collect as much, the challengers said, so the law is underinclusive. TikTok’s expert said it’s unlikely China would use TikTok for intelligence, because it has better ways. TikTok also says it already stores US data with Oracle.

Rests on

The law’s coverage. A declaration by TikTok’s expert that “it is unlikely that China would seek to compel TikTok to turn over user data for intelligence-gathering purposes.” Project Texas.

TikTok’s brief pp. 41–44, 50 · Creators’ brief p. 18

Authority

Brown v. Entertainment Merchants (2011), on underinclusive laws suggesting a different motive.

Where it is weak

Nobody disputed the interest itself. “Petitioners do not dispute” it, the Court wrote. “Nor could they.” And the view that China wouldn’t bother was TikTok’s own expert’s, set against the intelligence agencies’ declarations.

Opinion p. 13

Government

The argument

China has spent years building profiles of Americans, and TikTok’s data would give it “a powerful tool for harassment, recruitment, and espionage.” That includes people who never joined, through users’ contact lists. Chinese law requires companies like ByteDance to help its intelligence work, in secret.

Rests on

Declarations from the intelligence agencies, the FBI and the Justice Department. China’s national security laws. Reporting that ByteDance employees used TikTok data to track journalists.

Tr. pp. 117–118, 131, 146 · Government’s brief pp. 29–37

Authority

Holder v. Humanitarian Law Project (2010), on deference to the government in national security.

Where it is weak

Most of what it said China could do with the data is prediction, not something shown to have happened through TikTok in the US.

Opinion p. 14

Logic chainhow the reasoning runs, and where it breaks
  1. Test

    Is keeping the data from China an important enough reason for this law?

  2. Government

    China wants the data, can force ByteDance to hand it over, and has stolen data before.

  3. The data

    Real, but this law covers too little of it to be about it.

    The data

    Real, and TikTok’s scale makes it unique.

  4. Challengers

    A worthy goal this law doesn’t really serve.

  5. Where it lands

    Enough on its own, the Supreme Court held.

4May the government act because China might secretly steer what Americans see?

Challengers

No. Restricting speech because of what it might persuade people of is what the First Amendment forbids. “Mere ideas do not constitute a national security threat.” If secrecy is the worry, a warning label would answer it.

Government

Yes. The harm isn’t any message, it’s a foreign adversary’s power to manipulate a platform millions use, in secret, “in whatever form that kind of covert operation might take.” A warning can’t fix what can’t be seen.

CourtNot decided. The Supreme Court rested on data alone and didn’t say whether this reason is allowed. Justice Gorsuch was glad it didn’t endorse it.

Tr. p. 74 · TikTok’s brief pp. 35–41 · Tr. p. 118 · Government’s brief pp. 37–42 · Opinion pp. 17–19

Detailthe argument, what it rests on, where it is weak

Challengers

The argument

The manipulation reason is either a plain attempt to stop Americans hearing speech that might help a foreign country, which is forbidden, or a worry about secrecy that a disclosure would fix. The creators traced the history. America has answered foreign propaganda by labeling it, not banning it.

Rests on

Lamont v. Postmaster General (1965), on the right to receive foreign political material. Meese v. Keene (1987), upholding labels. The Foreign Agents Registration Act, which requires disclosure, not silence.

TikTok’s brief pp. 35–41 · Creators’ brief pp. 16–17 · Tr. pp. 95–96

Authority

Brandeis’s concurrence in Whitney v. California (1927), that the answer to bad speech is more speech.

Where it is weak

The government says it isn’t worried about ideas, but about who controls the feed, and secret control can’t be labeled away. Justice Kagan doubted the label would add much, because “everybody does know now that there’s China behind it.”

Tr. p. 123

Government

The argument

China has run covert influence operations on social media before, and ByteDance has manipulated content in other countries, sometimes for China. Control of TikTok gives China “a potent weapon for covert influence operations.” The concern is the capability, whatever the content, and disclosure is no remedy for something covert by definition.

Rests on

Intelligence declarations about China’s influence operations. Examples of ByteDance content changes abroad.

Government’s brief p. 18, 37–42 · Tr. p. 118

Authority

The tradition of barring foreign control of broadcasters. Humanitarian Law Project.

Where it is weak

Justice Gorsuch: “One man’s ‘covert content manipulation’ is another’s ‘editorial discretion.’” Justice Gorsuch also asked if the worry was “a pretty paternalistic point of view.”

Gorsuch, J. p. 1 · Tr. p. 123

Logic chainhow the reasoning runs, and where it breaks
  1. Test

    Can a fear of covert foreign influence justify a law that burdens speech?

  2. Government

    The danger is the hidden power, not any message.

  3. The fix

    A label tells users what they need to know.

    The fix

    A label can’t reveal what’s secret.

  4. Challengers

    Fear of persuasion is exactly what the First Amendment rules out.

  5. Where it lands

    Left open. The Court didn’t rest on it.

5Was there a less drastic fix?

Challengers

Yes. The 90-page security agreement, with Oracle holding the data and checking the code and a kill switch for the government. Or a ban on TikTok Inc. sharing users’ data with anyone, “under the threat of massive penalties.” Congress never even considered them.

Government

No. The agreement still let data flow to China and left ByteDance in control, and the government couldn’t trust ByteDance or watch it closely enough. Its experts said monitoring well enough to catch manipulation would be impossible. Only a sale cuts the tie.

CourtNo. The law is “a conditional ban,” and the alternatives “do not alter our tailoring analysis.” The D.C. Circuit called the disputes over the agreement “a matter of judgment, not of fact.”

Tr. pp. 172–173 · TikTok’s brief pp. 44–47 · Government’s brief p. 17 · Opinion pp. 16–17 · D.C. Cir. opinion p. 50

Detailthe argument, what it rests on, where it is weak

Challengers

The argument

Under any test, a law that burdens speech has to be a last resort. TikTok offered a detailed national security agreement, built much of it as Project Texas, and offered more. A flat ban on sharing sensitive data, a warning label, or the law’s own general track would each have done the job with less, and the record shows Congress didn’t weigh them.

Rests on

The draft agreement and TikTok’s declarations on Project Texas. The data-broker law passed in the same package, which bans sales of Americans’ data to foreign adversaries.

TikTok’s brief pp. 44–47 · Creators’ brief p. 18 · Tr. pp. 175–176

Authority

McCullen v. Coakley (2014), that the government must show it seriously considered less restrictive options.

Where it is weak

The government did consider the agreement, for two years, and turned it down. Courts give it room to judge what will work in national security.

D.C. Cir. opinion pp. 13–15, 49–53

Government

The argument

Under the agreement some US data would still go to China, ByteDance would still write the code, and the government would have to trust a company answerable to Beijing. Effective monitoring, it said, “would be impossible.” A divestiture is the only fix that ends China’s control, and it leaves the platform free to carry every video it carries now.

Rests on

The Justice Department’s declaration on the negotiations. The scale of TikTok’s code and how often it changes.

Government’s brief pp. 29–37 · D.C. Cir. opinion p. 15

Authority

Turner Broadcasting, that a law need not be the least restrictive means under intermediate scrutiny.

Where it is weak

The government chose the strictest option, and TikTok says it never explained why a strict data-sharing ban with heavy penalties wouldn’t work.

Tr. pp. 172–173

Logic chainhow the reasoning runs, and where it breaks
  1. Test

    Does a forced sale burden much more speech than the problem needs?

  2. Challengers

    A data ban or the security agreement would have done.

  3. The agreement

    Detailed, built, and checked by Oracle.

    The agreement

    Still left data flowing and ByteDance in charge.

  4. Government

    Only a sale ends China’s control.

  5. Where it lands

    A conditional ban, the Court held, and the alternatives didn’t change that.

6Why single out TikTok?

Challengers

Other apps tied to China collect as much. Temu has 70 million US users. And the law’s own general process, for any other foreign-controlled app, was there to use. Naming one speaker in a law calls for the hardest look.

Government

TikTok is unique, in its scale, in how much data it collects, and in how deeply it’s tied to a company China can command. Congress had studied it for years. Other apps can still be reached through the law’s general track.

CourtTikTok’s “special characteristics” justified treating it differently. But the Court stressed “the inherent narrowness of our holding”: a law aimed at another speaker would need its own look.

Tr. pp. 173–174 · TikTok’s brief pp. 51–56 · Government’s brief pp. 42–48 · Opinion pp. 12–13

Detailthe argument, what it rests on, where it is weak

Challengers

The argument

Congress gave every other app a process, a presidential finding with notice and a public report, and gave TikTok none. If the general track was enough for every other foreign-controlled app, it was enough for this one. Picking one platform by name, for reasons that include its content, is singling out a speaker.

Rests on

The law’s two tracks side by side. A federal commission’s report on Temu and Shein’s data practices.

TikTok’s brief pp. 51–56 · JA 339–343

Authority

Citizens United and Minneapolis Star v. Minnesota (1983), on laws aimed at particular speakers.

Where it is weak

Courts let Congress deal with the biggest problem first. The D.C. Circuit said the law singled TikTok out “because of its known characteristics and history.”

D.C. Cir. opinion p. 59

Government

The argument

TikTok is not just any app. Its scale, the data it gathers and ByteDance’s ties to China put it in a class of its own, and the executive branch had been trying to fix it since 2020. Congress may address the most serious threat directly, and the general track still covers others.

Rests on

The history of the 2020 orders and the CFIUS negotiations. The House report.

Government’s brief pp. 42–48 · H. Rep. 118-417

Authority

Turner Broadcasting, that a law may treat one kind of speaker differently for reasons unrelated to content.

Where it is weak

The Court made a point of limiting what this proves. “A law targeting any other speaker would by necessity entail a distinct inquiry and separate considerations.”

Opinion pp. 12–13

Logic chainhow the reasoning runs, and where it breaks
  1. Test

    Does TikTok’s difference justify treating it differently from other apps?

  2. Challengers

    Other apps collect as much, and a general process existed.

  3. TikTok

    One platform named in a law, with no process at all.

    TikTok

    The largest and most tied to China, studied for years.

  4. Government

    Congress may tackle the biggest threat first.

  5. Where it lands

    Justified by its “special characteristics,” in a ruling the Court kept narrow.

7Could TikTok really be sold in 270 days?

Challengers

No. Splitting the US app from the global one isn’t “commercially, technologically, or legally feasible,” and China’s export rules block a sale of the algorithm. So the law isn’t really a sale requirement. It’s a ban.

Government

Yes, if ByteDance wanted to. Social media companies can change hands fast, and Elon Musk bought Twitter “in about six months.” ByteDance had been on notice since 2020, and a sale after the deadline still lifts the ban.

CourtThe D.C. Circuit called 270 days “a substantial amount of time,” noted a later sale would still lift the ban, and blamed China’s export rules, not the law, for the difficulty.

TikTok’s petition ¶ 26 · Tr. p. 169 · D.C. Cir. opinion p. 46, 64

Detailthe argument, what it rests on, where it is weak

Challengers

The argument

TikTok’s US app runs on code shared with the rest of the world and on a global pool of videos. Rebuilding that for a new owner would take years. China has said it won’t let the algorithm be exported, so no buyer could get it. A sale on this clock was never a real option.

Rests on

TikTok’s declarations on how the platform is built. China’s export-control rules, which cover algorithms like TikTok’s, and its government’s statements that it wouldn’t allow a sale of the recommendation engine.

TikTok’s petition ¶¶ 26–30 · Tr. pp. 23–25

Authority

The law’s 270 days, § 2(a)(2), and its rule that a later sale still lifts the ban, § 2(c).

Where it is weak

The D.C. Circuit said the obstacle was China’s own rules. And as it turned out, a sale did happen, after the deadline, with the algorithm licensed rather than sold.

D.C. Cir. opinion p. 64 · TikTok USDS Joint Venture, Jan 23, 2026

Government

The argument

There’s “no inherent impediment to divesting a social media company.” TikTok has known since 2020 that the government wanted a sale. And the law doesn’t end at the deadline: if ByteDance sells later, the prohibitions “shall cease to apply.”

Rests on

The record of the 2020 order to sell. The law’s own text on a sale after the deadline.

Tr. p. 169 · Pub. L. 118-50 § 2(c)

Authority

The law itself, § 2(c)(1).

Where it is weak

No sale did happen before the deadline, and the one that came later took a year of orders not to enforce the law.

EO 14352

Logic chainhow the reasoning runs, and where it breaks
  1. Test

    Is the way out of the ban a real one?

  2. Challengers

    Not commercially, technically or legally feasible in time.

  3. The clock

    China blocks the algorithm, so no buyer can get it.

    The clock

    Twitter sold in six months, and a late sale still counts.

  4. Government

    ByteDance could sell if it chose to.

  5. Where it lands

    A real way out, the D.C. Circuit said, and a sale did close a year late.

8Should the Court have paused the law?

Challengers

Yes. At least an administrative stay or a short injunction, to decide something this big carefully. President-elect Trump asked for the deadline to be put off so he could find “a negotiated resolution.”

Government

No. A court can’t stop a law it hasn’t found likely unconstitutional, and Congress chose the deadline on purpose. Senator McConnell put it bluntly: “delay is the point.”

CourtNo pause. The Court decided the whole case a week after argument and dismissed the requests to pause the law as moot. The D.C. Circuit had refused too.

Tr. p. 6, 176 · Trump amicus brief p. 4 · McConnell amicus brief p. 2 · Journal p. 313

Detailthe argument, what it rests on, where it is weak

Challengers

The argument

The Court had less than a month for one of the biggest free-speech cases in years. An injunction or administrative stay would have given it time, and, as Francisco said at argument, might let the new President “potentially moot the case.”

Rests on

The short time allowed. The President-elect’s brief.

Tr. p. 6, 176 · Application, 24A587

Authority

The Court’s power to pause a law while it decides a case.

Where it is weak

The D.C. Circuit couldn’t find one case where a court, after upholding a law, had stopped it from taking effect pending Supreme Court review.

Order of Dec 13, 2024 p. 2

Government

The argument

The law already gave nine months, and Congress chose that clock deliberately. TikTok couldn’t show it was likely to win, which is what a pause needs. A pause asked for at the end of one administration, hoping the next will be kinder, is a play for time.

Rests on

The D.C. Circuit’s unanimous ruling. The law’s timetable.

Tr. pp. 164–165 · McConnell amicus brief p. 2

Authority

The usual test for a pause, which requires a likelihood of success.

Where it is weak

The Court itself was short of time. It had “a fortnight,” Justice Gorsuch wrote, and could sketch “only a few, and admittedly tentative, observations.”

Gorsuch, J. p. 1

Logic chainhow the reasoning runs, and where it breaks
  1. Test

    Should the law be held off while the Court decides?

  2. Challengers

    Time to decide carefully, and maybe a negotiated end.

  3. The clock

    Too little time for a case this big.

    The clock

    Congress chose it, and delay was the goal.

  4. Government

    No pause without a likely win.

  5. Where it lands

    No pause. The Court decided the case in a week instead.

9Could the courts rely on secret evidence?

Challengers

No. TikTok can’t answer evidence it isn’t allowed to see. The court should strike it, or at least have a special master with clearance review it.

Government

The public record is enough, but the classified record “describes the threat landscape in more detail and thus provides further support” for the law.

CourtNeither court used it. The D.C. Circuit decided “solely on the unredacted, public filings,” and the Supreme Court on “the public record, without reference to the classified evidence.”

TikTok’s opposition, Aug 5, 2024 · Government’s brief p. 19 · D.C. Cir. opinion p. 64 · Opinion p. 13 n. 3

Detailthe argument, what it rests on, where it is weak

Challengers

The argument

Due process means a party sees the case against it. A court ruling on evidence one side never saw can’t test it. If the court needed it at all, a special master with clearance could have reviewed it and argued TikTok’s side.

Rests on

The government’s motion to file classified declarations for the judges alone.

TikTok’s opposition, Aug 5, 2024 · Tr. pp. 25–26, 96–97

Authority

Due process cases on secret evidence.

Where it is weak

Neither court relied on it, so the objection never changed anything.

D.C. Cir. opinion pp. 64–65

Government

The argument

The law can be upheld on the public record, and was. The classified material only adds detail to the same threats, and courts have reviewed classified evidence in national security cases before.

Rests on

Classified declarations from intelligence officials, filed under seal.

Government’s brief pp. 48–50 · Motion of Jul 26, 2024

Authority

Holder v. Humanitarian Law Project and cases allowing review of classified material.

Where it is weak

Justice Gorsuch: “we have no business considering the government’s secret evidence here.”

Gorsuch, J. p. 2

Logic chainhow the reasoning runs, and where it breaks
  1. Test

    May a court decide on evidence one side can’t see?

  2. Challengers

    Not without a fair chance to answer it.

  3. The record

    Secret evidence can’t be tested.

    The record

    The public record is enough on its own.

  4. Government

    It only adds detail.

  5. Where it lands

    Not used by either court.

10Is the 2026 sale the “qualified divestiture” the law demands?

Challengers

No. ByteDance still owns the algorithm and licenses it, still runs the new company’s e-commerce, advertising and marketing, and TikTok’s global chief executive sits on its board. That’s an “operational relationship,” which the law forbids. (The Tan petitioners, a different set of challengers.)

Government

The government hasn’t argued this yet. It says the case should be dismissed first, as filed too late and by people with no standing. The President found on September 25, 2025 that the deal “precludes any ‘operational relationship.’”

CourtNothing decided. The motion to dismiss was referred to the merits panel on July 20, 2026, and the government’s record index was due October 20, 2026.

Amended petition pp. 1–3 · Motion to dismiss pp. 1–2 · EO 14352 § 2(b) · Order of Jul 20, 2026 · Order of Aug 21, 2026

Detailthe argument, what it rests on, where it is weak

Challengers

The argument

The law’s definition is strict: no operational relationship, “including any cooperation with respect to the operation of a content recommendation algorithm.” A licensed algorithm is exactly that, the challengers say, and so are shared software, staff, and features launched jointly. They also say the buyers had given to the President’s political projects, and a higher offer was turned down.

Rests on

The joint venture’s own announcement that TikTok’s global companies “will manage” e-commerce, advertising and marketing. A former ByteDance employee’s declaration. Press reports.

Amended petition pp. 3–6, 40–41 · TikTok USDS Joint Venture, Jan 23, 2026

Authority

The law’s definition of a qualified divestiture, § 2(g)(6).

Where it is weak

These are claims, and none has been tested. The court may never reach them if it decides the challengers sued too late or can’t sue.

Motion to dismiss pp. 1–2

Government

The argument

Before the merits, two thresholds. The law gives 90 days to sue after a government action, and the last one was September 25, 2025. And owning stock in a rival, or a lost job, isn’t the kind of injury that lets someone challenge the deal. On the deal itself, the President’s findings are that ByteDance holds under 20 percent and the new company controls the algorithms, code and moderation.

Rests on

The President’s order and its findings. The Justice Department’s 2026 opinion that the new company “functions independently of ByteDance.”

Motion to dismiss pp. 1–2 · Reply pp. 1–3 · EO 14352 · OLC opinion, Jul 16, 2026

Authority

The law’s 90-day deadline, § 3. The Supreme Court’s standing cases.

Where it is weak

The challengers say the September order was only conditional, “once its implementation agreements are executed,” so the clock couldn’t start before the sale closed in January 2026.

Opposition pp. 7–10

  2. Challengers

    ByteDance still licenses the algorithm and runs parts of the business.

  3. The sale

    A licensed algorithm is an operational relationship.

    The sale

? Terms

Plain English

Words these pages use because the courts use them. The case went through two courts, the D.C. Circuit and the Supreme Court, and a few words only matter after the ruling, in the case that came after it.

The courts

D.C. Circuit
The U.S. Court of Appeals for the District of Columbia Circuit, in Washington. The law sends every challenge to it first, and to no other court.
Panel
The three judges of an appeals court who hear a case or a motion. The D.C. Circuit has about a dozen judges, and different panels handle different steps.
Merits panel
The panel that will decide a case itself, as opposed to the ones that handle motions along the way.
Attorney General
The head of the Justice Department. The law gives this office alone the job of enforcing it, which is why the case is named after one.
Solicitor General
The lawyer who argues the federal government’s cases in the Supreme Court. Elizabeth Prelogar held the job when this case was argued.
Per curiam
Latin for “by the court.” An opinion that no one justice signs, issued in the name of the whole Court.
Concurring opinion
A justice who agrees with the result writing separately to give other or extra reasons. Concurring “in the judgment” means agreeing with the result but not the Court’s reasoning.

The paperwork

Docket
The court’s numbered list of everything filed in a case. Entry 42 is the forty-second thing filed.
Petition for review
How a case under this law starts: a request made straight to the appeals court, with no trial court first.
Exclusive jurisdiction
Being the only court allowed to hear a kind of case. The law gave that to the D.C. Circuit.
Consolidated
Separate cases joined so they’re briefed, argued and decided together.
Brief
A side’s written argument to the court.
Amicus brief
A brief from someone who isn’t a party, a “friend of the court,” giving the court a view of the case.
Declaration
A written statement a witness signs under penalty of perjury. There was no trial here, so the evidence was mostly declarations.
Record
Everything a court has in front of it to decide a case: the filings, the declarations, the evidence.
Classified evidence
Material the government keeps secret for national security. It filed some here for the judges alone, which the other side never got to see or answer.
Special master
Someone a court appoints to help with a task. TikTok asked for one with a security clearance to review the secret evidence.

How the case moved

Application
A request to the Supreme Court for emergency help before it has taken a case. Here, to pause the law before it took effect.
Injunction
A court order stopping something. One “pending review” pauses a law while a higher court looks at the case.
Certiorari
The Supreme Court agreeing to hear a case. Here it treated TikTok’s emergency applications as requests for it and granted them two days later.
Question presented
The exact question the Supreme Court agrees to answer. Here there was one: does the law, as applied to TikTok and the creators, violate the First Amendment.
As applied
A challenge to how a law works on the people bringing it, not to every possible use of it. The Supreme Court looked only at the part of the law that names TikTok.
Oral argument
The time the lawyers spend answering the judges’ questions in court. No witnesses, no new evidence and no jury.
Divided argument
Two lawyers splitting one side’s time. TikTok’s lawyer and the creators’ lawyer shared the challengers’ side.
Rebuttal
The few minutes the side that brought the case gets at the end, to answer the other side.
Mandate
The formal order that closes an appeal and makes the decision final in the court below.
Moot
No longer a live question, so there’s nothing left for a court to decide.

The law

Divestiture
Selling off a business, or enough of it that the seller no longer controls it.
Qualified divestiture
A sale the President finds ends a foreign adversary’s control and leaves no “operational relationship” with ByteDance. Only that lifts the law’s ban.
Operational relationship
The tie the law says can’t survive the sale, “including any cooperation with respect to the operation of a content recommendation algorithm or an agreement with respect to data sharing.”
Foreign adversary
A country the law treats as one: China, Russia, Iran and North Korea.
App stores and hosting
What the law actually forbids, which is offering the app in an app store or hosting it. So it binds companies like Apple, Google and Oracle, not TikTok’s users.
Civil penalty
A fine, not a crime. The law sets it at up to $5,000 for each US user reached through a violation.
The one extension
The law let the President add up to 90 days, once, if he told Congress a sale was in sight and showed binding agreements. No President used it.
The general track
The law’s second route, which lets the President name other apps controlled by a foreign adversary later. TikTok was named in the law itself.
Recommendation algorithm
The software that picks which video each user sees next on the “For You” feed. ByteDance owns it, and it’s developed and maintained in China.
Executive order
A written order from the President to the executive branch, published in the Federal Register.
Non-enforcement
The executive branch choosing not to bring cases under a law, here on the President’s orders. The law itself stays on the books.
CFIUS
The Committee on Foreign Investment in the United States, the federal panel that reviews foreign purchases of US businesses for security risks.
National security agreement
A contract setting the security conditions a company operates under. TikTok’s draft ran about 90 pages and was never signed.
Project Texas
TikTok’s plan to keep US users’ data in the United States, stored with Oracle and run by a separate US company. TikTok says it spent more than $2 billion on it.
IEEPA
The International Emergency Economic Powers Act, the 1977 law President Trump used for his 2020 ban. Courts found it couldn’t reach this kind of communication.

The free-speech test

First Amendment
The part of the Constitution that bars the government from “abridging the freedom of speech.” Courts check laws that burden speech with one of a few tests.
Assume without deciding
A court treating something as true for the sake of argument, because the answer wouldn’t change the result, and leaving the real question open.
Heightened scrutiny
Any of the stricter tests a court uses when a law burdens speech, instead of just asking whether the law is reasonable.
Strict scrutiny
The hardest test. The government needs a compelling reason and the narrowest way of getting there. Most laws that get it fail.
Intermediate scrutiny
The middle test. The government needs an important reason that isn’t about suppressing speech, and a law that doesn’t burden much more speech than it has to.
Content-based
Aimed at what is said, its topic, idea or message. A content-based law gets strict scrutiny.
Content-neutral
Applying whatever is said, like a limit on how loud a speaker can be. A content-neutral law gets intermediate scrutiny.
Singling out a speaker
A law aimed at one speaker. Courts look harder when that’s a way of getting at what the speaker says.
Tailoring
Whether a law fits its reason, covering what the problem needs and not much more.
Less restrictive alternative
Another way of meeting the same goal that burdens less speech. If one would work as well, the law can fail.
Underinclusive
Covering too little of the problem a law claims to fix, which can suggest the stated reason isn’t the real one.
Deference
A court giving weight to another branch’s judgment, here Congress’s predictions about national security.
Covert content manipulation
The government’s second reason: that China could secretly have ByteDance change what Americans see on TikTok. The Supreme Court didn’t rest on it.

The other claims

Equal protection
The Constitution’s promise that the government won’t treat people differently without a good enough reason.
Bill of attainder
A law that punishes a named person or group without a trial. The Constitution forbids Congress to pass one.
Taking
The government seizing private property, which the Constitution allows only with fair payment.

The case after the case

Standing
The right to bring a case at all. A challenger has to show a real injury of their own, caused by what they’re challenging, that a court could fix.
Deadline to sue
The time limit for bringing a case, also called a statute of limitations. This law allows 90 days after the government action being challenged.
Motion to dismiss
Asking a court to end a case without deciding who is right, for a reason like filing too late or having no standing.
Ripe
Ready for a court. A challenge to something that hasn’t happened yet comes too early.
Pseudonym
A made-up name, here “John Doe,” that a court lets a party use to keep their identity off the public record.
Certified index
The government’s official list of what’s in the record behind the decision being challenged. Briefing usually follows it.
Motion to govern
The parties’ proposal to the court for how a case should go on from here.
FOIA
The Freedom of Information Act, which lets anyone ask a federal agency for its records, and sue if it refuses.

The stamp on a ruling

Granted
The court said yes to the request on this card, whichever side made it.
Denied
The court said no to the request on this card.
Affirmed
The court above agreed with the court below and left its ruling as it was.
Referred
The court didn’t decide the request yet. It passed it on to the judges who will decide the whole case.
