Yes. The video was made in Miami on September 12, 2020, and nobody in the case has ever said it was recorded without Doe’s OK. Not Doe, not Steven, and not the court. Fair, but the record does answer this one.

The record shows · No

No, and nobody in the case says she did, Steven included. At an evidentiary hearing on June 3, 2025, Steven agreed he has nothing in writing from Doe saying he could share it with other people. Asked whether he ever asked her before sending it to “Rose,” he said that if they talked about it, it would have been in person when they recorded it, and nothing after that he’s aware of.

What Steven argues is that it was implied. The two of them had spent about a year swapping explicit messages and videos of themselves, and in his sworn declaration he says Doe never objected to him showing the video to his fiancée at the time, “or to anyone else.” Doe agrees she didn’t object to the fiancée, but in her own declaration she says she “was very clear that they should not be shared with anyone else.” The trouble for that argument is the federal law itself. It says agreeing to be recorded doesn’t count as agreeing to have it shared, and it defines consent as an “affirmative, conscious, and voluntary” OK, which not objecting isn’t. The court hasn’t ruled on consent yet.

On her side, Doe points to a message she sent Steven in early 2020, before they ever met up, saying that if anyone asked about them having sex she would “rather deny and keep that private between us.”

Question 3 of 10

Was this revenge porn?

Your answer to question 3

Same as the court. It hasn’t decided either.

The court hasn’t decided this one. In the broad sense a lot of people mean now, though, you’re close.

The court hasn’t decided this one. In the everyday sense, though, you’ve got a point.

The court hasn’t decided

It depends what you mean by it, and the part that counts in court hasn’t been settled.

What people usually mean by it. Someone, usually an ex, posting or spreading a partner’s intimate images to humiliate them or get back at them. By that measure the record leans toward no:

  • The two of them were friends, not exes getting back at each other. They were still working together on getting the video taken down in December 2024.
  • Steven sent the video privately to one person who asked for it. He never posted it anywhere public.
  • It reached the public through a stranger’s leak.
  • Even Doe’s own complaint never says he did it to get back at her. It says he shared it on purpose, without her consent, and “for no legitimate purpose.”

A lot of people now also use the term for any intimate video shared without consent, whatever the reason. By that broader meaning the record is closer to yes, since Steven admits he sent it and doesn’t say she ever told him he could. That split is why many groups that work on this now say “nonconsensual intimate images” instead.

What the law says. No law in this case uses the words “revenge porn”:

  • The federal law doesn’t care about motive at all. It only asks whether someone shared the image knowing, or not caring, that the person in it hadn’t agreed. It also didn’t exist yet in April 2022.
  • Florida’s law, the way it read when Steven sent the video, only covered sharing done “with the intent of causing substantial emotional distress” to the person in the image. That’s about as close as the law gets to “revenge.” Florida dropped that part in 2025, but the law as it read back then is the one that applies to what was done back then.

Whether Steven broke either law is exactly what the court hasn’t decided yet.

Sources ECF No. 120 ¶¶ 51–53 · ECF No. 51-3 · 15 U.S.C. § 6851(b)(1)(A) · Fla. Stat. § 784.049(2)(c) (2022)

No. Steven sent the video privately, over Discord, to one account. It went public on November 29, 2024, when someone posting as “SoloTinyLeaks” put up a cache of 29 of Steven’s private files on a forum, and this video was one of them. From there it spread to porn sites with both of their screen names in the captions. Neither of them agreed to any of that.

Her complaint says he shared his videos through Google Drive links that anyone with the link could open, and that the leaker said that’s where the videos came from. Steven says the leak happened without his knowledge or consent, and in the weeks after it he was hunting down site contacts and sending takedown requests, with Doe sending him more links to go after.

Question 6 of 10

Did Steven knowingly send the video to a minor?

Your answer to question 6

Same as the record.

The record says otherwise.

Fair, but the record does answer this one.

The record shows · No

No, on both halves. Steven believed he was talking to a college student. And the person actually running the account was that boyfriend from the last question, who the account’s real owner said was 21 when she met him in November 2021. Doe’s own complaint described “Rose” as “a 19-year old woman.”

So where does the minor claim come from? The account was pretending to be a real girl, and she testified she was 17 when she met that man. In October 2025, three weeks before her deposition, Doe’s lawyers wrote in a footnote that the person Steven sent the video to was a minor, going off a redacted driving record. The deposition then showed the person on the other end was the man, not her.

Doe’s side never filed a correction. Steven asked the court to punish them for it under Rule 11, and in April 2026 the court turned that down without prejudice, which leaves either side free to file again. He has also asked that a jury never hear the claim, in a motion in limine that’s still undecided.

Question 7 of 10

Can Steven be sued under federal law just for sending the video in April 2022?

Your answer to question 7

Same as the record.

The record says otherwise.

Fair, but the record does answer this one.

The record shows · No

No. The federal law Doe is suing under started on October 1, 2022, and a law normally isn’t retroactive, meaning it doesn’t reach back to things done before it existed. Steven sent the video to “Rose” on April 9, 2022, and Doe agreed to that date in May 2026, so that send happened about six months too early to count by itself.

This federal law doesn’t make anything a crime, either. It’s the kind of law that lets someone sue. And Florida’s own law was already in force in April 2022, so Doe’s three Florida claims don’t depend on this date. They’re only in federal court because of the federal claim, though, so if that one goes, the judge will most likely leave them for a Florida court.

That’s why Doe’s federal claim now rides on one of two other routes. One is the November 2023 send in the next question. The other is her argument that the link Steven sent in April 2022 stayed open after the law started, and that leaving it open counts as sharing it after October 1, 2022. The court hasn’t ruled on either one.

Sources 15 U.S.C. § 6851 · ECF No. 247 at 1, 3, 20 · ECF No. 120 ¶ 45 · ECF Nos. 132, 210

Question 8 of 10

Has anyone sworn that Steven sent them the video after the law took effect?

Your answer to question 8

Same as the record.

The record says otherwise.

Fair, but the record does answer this one.

The record shows · Yes

Yes. A woman known in the case as “Abbymc” signed a sworn declaration in May 2025 saying Steven sent her the video through a Google Drive link in November 2023, and that she recognized Doe in it. That’s well after the law started, so if it’s true, it’s enough for Doe’s federal claim on its own.

Steven has denied it from the start, and the declaration came with no message, link or file to back it up. There is a message she sent a friend on the morning of November 3, 2023, at 10:41 a.m., saying Steven had just sent her another video. Steven’s own log puts the only links he sent her that day at 2:39 p.m., which looks like a four hour gap. But at his deposition he said he wasn’t sure what time zone the log uses, and if it’s universal time, 2:39 p.m. would be 10:39 a.m. in Miami, two minutes before her message.

When Steven’s side served her with a subpoena for more, she handed over nothing, and a second declaration from her showed up after the deadline for gathering evidence had passed. Whether to believe her is one of the things the court still has to decide.

? Terms

Plain English

Words this page uses because the court uses them. Nothing here is special to this case.

The paperwork

Docket
The court’s numbered list of everything filed in a case. Entry 42 is the forty-second thing filed.
ECF No.
How a filing is cited: its docket number. A number after a dash, like 42-4, is an attachment to it — usually an exhibit.
Complaint
The document that starts a lawsuit and sets out what the plaintiff says happened. An amended complaint replaces it.
Declaration
A written statement signed under penalty of perjury. It counts as evidence without the witness appearing in person.
Exhibit
A document, screenshot or record attached to a filing as proof of something asserted in it.
Transcript
The word-for-word record of a hearing or a deposition. Cited by page and line, so 55:7 means page 55, line 7.

How a case moves

Motion
A formal request that the court do something. The other side files an opposition; the moving side may then file a reply.
Discovery
The stage where each side must hand over relevant evidence to the other. It has a deadline, and missing it matters.
Deposition
Sworn question-and-answer, out of court, recorded by a court reporter. Lawyers for both sides attend; there is no judge.
Subpoena
A court order compelling someone who is not a party to hand over documents or give testimony.
Evidentiary hearing
A hearing where witnesses actually testify and are cross-examined, rather than lawyers simply arguing.
Injunction
A court order to do something or to stop doing it. A temporary restraining order is the emergency version, and a preliminary injunction lasts while the case goes on. Neither decides who is right.
Magistrate judge
A judge who handles pre-trial matters. On some questions they recommend, and the district judge decides.
Paperless order
A short ruling typed straight onto the docket. It has no document behind it, so its citation opens the docket entry instead.

The motions that can end things

Motion in limine
Filed before trial to decide what a jury will and will not be allowed to hear.
Spoliation
Destroying or losing evidence you were obliged to keep. The duty starts once you know a lawsuit is coming.
Rule 11
The rule requiring that filings have a factual basis. A Rule 11 motion says the other side asserted something it could not support.

Tests the court applies

Retroactive
Whether a law reaches conduct from before it existed. Usually it does not, unless Congress clearly said so.
