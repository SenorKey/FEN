  1. The spoliation motion

    The case is here

    Decided first, and apart from the rest

    Should he lose the case for destroying evidence?

    Undecided

    Spoliation means destroying or losing evidence you were supposed to keep. The plaintiff says the defendant did that. She says he deleted his side of conversations after he was told to preserve them, and that a log he swore listed everything he sent one witness is missing two links. So she filed a motion, which is a formal request to the judge, asking for him to be penalised for it. He says nothing was actually lost, and that she filed it too late.

    The judge is deciding this first, and everything below it is on hold until he does. The most she asked for is a ruling that he is legally responsible without a trial on it, and that’s the yes on this card. If it goes that way, the question below never has to be answered at all. Anything less is a no here, and the case just moves down to that question. That covers the judge siding with the defendant, throwing the motion out for being late, or picking a smaller penalty, like the jury being told it can assume the missing evidence was bad for him.

    If yes

    Outcome

    Judgment against him

    Then

    Decided in her favour without a trial

    This is the most she asked for. The judge would rule that he is legally responsible without a trial on it, as the penalty for losing evidence he was supposed to keep. The rule she is relying on only allows that if he got rid of it on purpose, so that she couldn’t use it.

    If it went this way, the argument over what happened would be over, and in her favour. The date question in the middle of this page would never have to be answered.

    If no

  2. The only question

    Did he disclose the video after 1 October 2022?

    Stayed until that ruling

    This is the question the whole case comes down to. The federal law she is suing under took effect on 1 October 2022, and it can’t be used for anything that happened before that. Both sides now agree he sent the video to someone in April 2022, which is about six months too early. So she has to show that he disclosed it again at some point after that date. Disclose is the law’s own word, and it covers sending something, publishing it, or making it accessible.

    If she can’t show that, there is no federal claim, and a federal court has no jurisdiction, meaning no power to hear the case at all. She has two different ways of trying to show it, and those are the two routes inside this card. A yes on either one makes this a yes. Right now this question is stayed, which means the judge has put it on hold until the spoliation motion above is decided.

    Who must prove itShe does. The burden is on the plaintiff, and she has to meet it by a preponderance of the evidence, which just means more likely than not.

    A yes on either one is enough

    Route A · The November 2023 transmission

    Could a jury credit an untested declaration?

    Undecided

    This is her first way of getting past that date. She says he sent the video a second time in November 2023, to a witness who goes by “Abbymc” online. That would be thirteen months after the law took effect, so if it happened, it counts. He says it never happened, and points to his own saved logs of what he sent her, where the video doesn’t show up.

    What she has is two declarations from Abbymc, which are sworn written statements, saying she received it. She also has a message Abbymc sent a friend that same morning. Whether that’s enough is the question this card asks.

    This is the test Route A has to pass. To credit a statement just means to believe it. A declaration does count as evidence, but Abbymc herself has never been questioned by either side. She never sat for a deposition, which is sworn questioning outside of court. She handed over nothing when she was sent a subpoena, which is a court order to produce documents or testify. And a few days after signing her first declaration she told the defendant she didn’t want to be involved anymore.

    So the judge has to decide if a reasonable jury could still believe her on paper alone. If it could, the two sides have a real disagreement about a fact, and only a trial can settle that. If it couldn’t, Route A is closed.

    or

    Route B · Continuing accessibility

    Does “make accessible” reach a private link?

    Undecided

    This is her second way, and it doesn’t need a second send at all. The law doesn’t only cover sending or publishing a video. It also covers making it accessible. The copy he sent in April 2022 stayed reachable until he deleted it in November 2024, and he has admitted that under oath.

    So her argument is that even though the sending happened too early, leaving it reachable went on for about two years after the law took effect, and that this counts as a disclosure all by itself.

    This is the test Route B has to pass, and it’s about what the words of the law mean, not about what happened. He never posted the video anywhere public himself. He sent it to one person privately, and even her own expert agreed the link was basically impossible to guess. The defendant says that isn’t making something accessible in any real sense, and that she is stretching one message from April 2022 into something that lasts for years so that a newer law can reach it. She says the law says “make accessible”, not “publish”, so it doesn’t have to be public.

    No court has applied this law to a private link like this before, so whichever way the judge goes, it will be a first. If she wins this one, the federal court keeps the case even if Route A fails.

    If yes

    Outcome

    Federal jurisdiction survives

    Then

    Trial on the merits

    If either route works, she has a disclosure after the date, and the federal court has jurisdiction, which is the power to hear the case at all. What comes next is a trial on the merits. That means a trial about what actually happened and who is in the right, instead of about whether this court can hear it.

    She would go into that trial in a strong position on the main issue, which is consent. He admits he sent the video, and he has testified that he never asked her. He says permission was implied by their earlier exchanges, but the law asks for consent that was actually given.

    If no

    Neither route · The April 2022 transmission only

    Outcome

    No federal claim

    Then

    Case ends here

    If neither route passes its test, the only sharing anyone has proven is the one from April 2022. That’s about six months before the law took effect. She used to argue it happened in October 2022, but in May 2026 she agreed that the real date was 9 April 2022.

    A law normally doesn’t apply retroactively, meaning to things that happened before it existed, so that one send can’t support a federal claim.

    With no disclosure after the date, there’s nothing left under the federal law, and a federal court can only keep a case that has a federal claim in it. She also has claims under state law, but those are only in this court because they came attached to the federal one. So when the federal claim goes, the state ones are dismissed along with it, and the case is over in this court.

    That wouldn’t be a ruling that he did nothing wrong. It would be a ruling that this court isn’t allowed to decide it.

? Terms

Plain English

Words this page uses because the court uses them. Nothing here is special to this case.

The paperwork

Docket
The court’s numbered list of everything filed in a case. Entry 42 is the forty-second thing filed.
ECF No.
How a filing is cited: its docket number. A number after a dash, like 42-4, is an attachment to it — usually an exhibit.
Complaint
The document that starts a lawsuit and sets out what the plaintiff says happened. An amended complaint replaces it.
Answer
The defendant’s formal response to the complaint, admitting or denying each allegation.
Declaration
A written statement signed under penalty of perjury. It counts as evidence without the witness appearing in person.
Exhibit
A document, screenshot or record attached to a filing as proof of something asserted in it.
Transcript
The word-for-word record of a hearing or a deposition. Cited by page and line, so 55:7 means page 55, line 7.
Errata
A formal correction a witness files to their own sworn testimony after reading it back.
Under seal
Filed with the court but kept off the public record. Both sides and the judge can read it; nobody else can.

How a case moves

Motion
A formal request that the court do something. The other side files an opposition; the moving side may then file a reply.
Discovery
The stage where each side must hand over relevant evidence to the other. It has a deadline, and missing it matters.
Deposition
Sworn question-and-answer, out of court, recorded by a court reporter. Lawyers for both sides attend; there is no judge.
Subpoena
A court order compelling someone who is not a party to hand over documents or give testimony.
Evidentiary hearing
A hearing where witnesses actually testify and are cross-examined, rather than lawyers simply arguing.
Protective order
An order limiting what either side may do with the material it receives in discovery.
Magistrate judge
A judge who handles pre-trial matters. On some questions they recommend, and the district judge decides.
Paperless order
A short ruling typed straight onto the docket. It has no document behind it, so its citation opens the docket entry instead.
Stay
A pause ordered by the court. Whatever is stayed sits on hold until something else is decided first.
Merits
What the case is actually about, meaning what happened and who is in the right. A ruling that a court cannot hear a case says nothing about the merits.

The motions that can end things

Motion to dismiss
Argues the case cannot proceed even if everything alleged is true — usually because the court lacks power to hear it.
Summary judgment
Argues the facts are not genuinely in dispute, so the judge can decide without a trial. If facts are disputed, it fails.
Motion in limine
Filed before trial to decide what a jury will and will not be allowed to hear.
Spoliation
Destroying or losing evidence you were obliged to keep. The duty starts once you know a lawsuit is coming.
Sanctions
A penalty for misconduct in the litigation itself. They can run from a fine to losing the case outright.
Adverse inference
A sanction where the jury is told it may assume the destroyed evidence would have hurt the side that destroyed it.
Rule 11
The rule requiring that filings have a factual basis. A Rule 11 motion says the other side asserted something it could not support.

Tests the court applies

Jurisdiction
Whether this court has the power to hear this case at all. A federal court needs a federal claim, which is why one date decides so much here.
Burden
Which side has to prove a thing. If they do not, they lose that point, even if the other side proves nothing either.
Preponderance of the evidence
More likely than not. It is the usual standard of proof in a civil case, and a lower bar than the criminal one.
Retroactive
Whether a law reaches conduct from before it existed. Usually it does not, unless Congress clearly said so.
Hearsay
Repeating what someone said outside court to prove it was true. Generally not allowed, with many exceptions.
Authentication
Showing a document or screenshot is genuinely what its proponent says it is. The bar is low, but it is not nothing.
Probative
Actually tending to prove something. Evidence can be admissible and still carry very little weight.
