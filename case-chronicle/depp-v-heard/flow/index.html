  1. His claim · Three sentences in the op-ed

    What he sued over

    Did the sentence defame him?

    Yes, for all three

    This is where his side of the case starts. On December 18, 2018, the Washington Post published an op-ed under Amber Heard’s name, about what happens to women who speak up about abuse. It never mentions Johnny Depp by name. He sued her for defamation anyway, which means he says it hurt his reputation by stating things about him that were false.

    He didn’t sue over the whole piece. He picked out three sentences from it, and one of those is the headline, which the newspaper wrote and not her. Each of the three goes through the four questions inside this card separately, and it needs a yes to all four.

    A yes to all four is needed

    Question 1

    Is it of and concerning the claimant?

    “Of and concerning” is the court’s way of asking if a statement is actually about the person suing over it. The claimant is whoever is suing over that statement, so that’s him for the op-ed sentences and her for the lawyer’s statements. The statement doesn’t have to use their name. It’s enough that people reading it, who know the background, would understand who it means.

    This question matters most for the op-ed, because it never names him. If the jury had decided those sentences weren’t about him, his case would have ended right here. A yes moves the statement on to question 2. A no ends it for that statement.

    and

    Question 2

    Is it a false statement of fact?

    There are really two things packed into this one. First, the statement has to be saying something as a fact, meaning something that can be shown to be true or false. An opinion can’t be defamation, no matter how harsh it is. Second, that fact has to be false. If what was said is true, the claim over that statement is finished, because the truth is a complete defense.

    For the op-ed, the jury wasn’t asked about the marriage in general. They were asked if what each of those three sentences said or implied about him was false.

    and

    Question 3

    Is it defamatory in meaning?

    A statement can be false and still not be defamation. To count, it has to be the kind of thing that damages a person’s reputation, the kind that makes people think less of them. And it doesn’t have to say it outright. What a statement implies counts just as much as what it says word for word.

    That’s the only reason an op-ed that never names him could go in front of a jury in the first place. His claim was about what those sentences implied about him, not what they literally said.

    and

    Question 4

    Was it published with actual malice?

    This is the hardest of the four to prove. Actual malice sounds like it’s about spite or bad intentions, but in court it means something more specific. It means the person put the statement out knowing it was false, or had serious doubts about it being true and put it out anyway.

    It’s only on the list because both of them are public figures, and the law makes it harder on purpose for well-known people to win a defamation case. It also has to be proven to a higher standard than usual. In most lawsuits “more likely than not” is enough. Here it has to be clear and convincing, which is a step above that.

    If yes

    All four yes

    The sentence is actionable

    The op-ed, scored

    Three of three

    Actionable just means the statement is something you can win a lawsuit over. A statement only gets here if the jury said yes to all four questions about it. Only the statements that make it this far can be counted when the jury decides how much money is owed, which is called damages.

    This is how his side came out. The jury said yes to all four questions on all three of the op-ed sentences, including the headline she didn’t write. They awarded him $10 million in compensatory damages, which is money to make up for the harm done, and another $5 million in punitive damages, which is money meant purely as punishment.

    Virginia puts a legal limit on punitive damages, so the judge cut that $5 million down to $350,000. That left him with $10.35 million.

    If no

    Any one no

    That sentence fails

    The op-ed, scored

    None of the three did

    The four questions aren’t a score where three out of four is good enough. A statement needs all four. If the jury says no to even one of them, that statement is out completely and no money can be awarded for it.

    But it’s only that one statement that is out. Each of the others gets its own run through the same four questions, so one failing doesn’t take the rest down with it.

    Either way

  2. Her counterclaim · Three statements by his lawyer

    What she counterclaimed over

    Did the statement defame her?

    Yes, for one of three

    This is where her side starts. After he sued her, she sued him back inside the same case, which is called a counterclaim. Hers isn’t about anything he said himself. It’s about three statements his lawyer, Adam Waldman, gave to the Daily Mail in April and June 2020, each one calling her abuse allegations a hoax.

    Her argument is that the lawyer was speaking for him, so he answers for what was said. Her three statements go through the same four questions as his three. That makes six statements in total, and that’s all the jury was ever asked to decide.

    If no

    Any one no

    That statement fails

    The lawyer’s statements, scored

    Two of three did

    Two of the lawyer’s three statements ended up here. They were the two that called her allegations a hoax in broader terms. A statement needs a yes to all four questions, and the jury didn’t give these two that, so no money could be awarded for them.

    It works the same way it does for his sentences. One statement failing doesn’t take the others down with it, which is how her third one could still succeed.

    If yes

    All four yes

    The statement is actionable

    The lawyer’s statements, scored

    One of three

    This is how her side came out. Only one of the lawyer’s three statements made it through all four questions. That was the one claiming she and her friends staged the penthouse between two 911 calls. The other two, which called her allegations a hoax in broader terms, failed. The jury awarded her $2 million in compensatory damages and nothing in punitive damages.

    So each of them was found to have defamed the other, but the amounts were nowhere close. He appealed this one finding and she filed an appeal as well. Both were dropped when the two of them settled, so no higher court ever reviewed any of it.

Nothing on this diagram asks whether the marriage was abusive, because no question on the verdict form did either.

Case Chronicle · frontendneeded.com

? Terms

Plain English

Words this page uses because the court uses them. Nothing here is special to this case.

The paperwork

Docket
The court’s numbered list of everything filed in a case. Entry 42 is the forty-second thing filed.
ECF No.
How a filing is cited: its docket number. A number after a dash, like 42-4, is an attachment to it — usually an exhibit.
Complaint
The document that starts a lawsuit and sets out what the plaintiff says happened. An amended complaint replaces it.
Answer
The defendant’s formal response to the complaint, admitting or denying each allegation.
Declaration
A written statement signed under penalty of perjury. It counts as evidence without the witness appearing in person.
Exhibit
A document, screenshot or record attached to a filing as proof of something asserted in it.
Transcript
The word-for-word record of a hearing or a deposition. Cited by page and line, so 55:7 means page 55, line 7.
Errata
A formal correction a witness files to their own sworn testimony after reading it back.
Under seal
Filed with the court but kept off the public record. Both sides and the judge can read it; nobody else can.

How a case moves

Motion
A formal request that the court do something. The other side files an opposition; the moving side may then file a reply.
Discovery
The stage where each side must hand over relevant evidence to the other. It has a deadline, and missing it matters.
Deposition
Sworn question-and-answer, out of court, recorded by a court reporter. Lawyers for both sides attend; there is no judge.
Subpoena
A court order compelling someone who is not a party to hand over documents or give testimony.
Evidentiary hearing
A hearing where witnesses actually testify and are cross-examined, rather than lawyers simply arguing.
Protective order
An order limiting what either side may do with the material it receives in discovery.
Magistrate judge
A judge who handles pre-trial matters. On some questions they recommend, and the district judge decides.
Paperless order
A short ruling typed straight onto the docket. It has no document behind it, which is why some entries here do not link.

The motions that can end things

Motion to dismiss
Argues the case cannot proceed even if everything alleged is true — usually because the court lacks power to hear it.
Summary judgment
Argues the facts are not genuinely in dispute, so the judge can decide without a trial. If facts are disputed, it fails.
Motion in limine
Filed before trial to decide what a jury will and will not be allowed to hear.
Spoliation
Destroying or losing evidence you were obliged to keep. The duty starts once you know a lawsuit is coming.
Sanctions
A penalty for misconduct in the litigation itself. They can run from a fine to losing the case outright.
Adverse inference
A sanction where the jury is told it may assume the destroyed evidence would have hurt the side that destroyed it.
Rule 11
The rule requiring that filings have a factual basis. A Rule 11 motion says the other side asserted something it could not support.

Tests the court applies

Jurisdiction
Whether this court has the power to hear this case at all. A federal court needs a federal claim, which is why one date decides so much here.
Burden
Which side has to prove a thing. If they do not, they lose that point, even if the other side proves nothing either.
Retroactive
Whether a law reaches conduct from before it existed. Usually it does not, unless Congress clearly said so.
Hearsay
Repeating what someone said outside court to prove it was true. Generally not allowed, with many exceptions.
Authentication
Showing a document or screenshot is genuinely what its proponent says it is. The bar is low, but it is not nothing.
Probative
Actually tending to prove something. Evidence can be admissible and still carry very little weight.

Defamation and damages

Defamation
A false statement of fact, put out to other people, that damages someone’s reputation.
Counterclaim
A claim the person being sued brings back against the person who sued them. Both are tried together as one case.
Of and concerning
About the person who is suing. The statement need not name them, as long as people who know the background would understand who it means.
Actual malice
Putting a statement out knowing it was false, or with serious doubts about whether it was true. It has nothing to do with spite. A public figure has to prove it.
Clear and convincing
A standard of proof above the usual civil one of more likely than not, and below the criminal one of beyond a reasonable doubt.
Actionable
Enough to win a lawsuit over. A statement that is not actionable cannot support a claim, however much it stung.
Damages
Money a court orders one side to pay the other.
Compensatory damages
Money meant to make up for harm actually done.
Punitive damages
Money awarded as punishment, on top of what makes up for the harm. Some states cap it by law, and Virginia is one.
